SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The literature at the intersection of artificial intelligence and sports betting can be organized around three principal strands. The first strand concerns **AI and machine learning methods applied to sports prediction and analytics**. Surveys by Beal et al. (2019) and Davis et al. (2023) formalize core analytical constructs such as expected game-state value, win probability, and team-strength measures that underpin quantitative sports modeling. These works establish that AI-driven prediction in sports has matured considerably, though their focus remains predominantly on performance analytics rather than on the betting ecosystem itself.

The second strand addresses **gambling harm, structural characteristics of sports betting, and responsible gambling tools**. The Conceptual Framework of Harmful Gambling (Abbott et al., 2013; 2018) and the Lancet Public Health Commission on gambling (Wardle et al., 2024) provide comprehensive, internationally grounded frameworks for understanding gambling-related harm across multiple dimensions. GAPS:
1. There is a near-total absence of research on **large language models (LLMs) and generative AI applied to sports betting**. While ChatGPT and related models have been studied in healthcare (Sallam, 2023), sports medicine (Fayed et al., 2023), and role-playing (Wang et al., 2024), no retrieved paper examines their use for generating betting tips, simulating bettor interactions, powering conversational betting assistants, or analyzing unstructured sports data (e.g., press conferences, injury reports) for betting intelligence.
2. **AI-driven personalized responsible gambling interventions in sports betting remain unexplored.** Although Auer & Griffiths (2019) used ML to predict limit-setting behavior, no study has designed, implemented, or evaluated an AI system that dynamically adapts responsible gambling messages, interventions, or friction mechanisms to individual bettor risk profiles in real time within sports betting platforms.
3. **The ethical and regulatory dimensions of AI use by sports betting operators are entirely absent from the literature.** No paper examines how operators deploy AI for odds manipulation, targeted promotional offers, micro-segmentation of bettors, or behavioral nudging, nor is there analysis of the regulatory frameworks needed to govern such practicesâdespite the Lancet Commission (Wardle et al., 2024) and Livingstone et al. (2019) calling for stronger policy interventions.
4. **Real-time AI systems for in-play betting risk management and integrity monitoring are underexplored.** While Parke & Parke (2019) identify in-play betting as a key structural risk factor, no study investigates AI systems that monitor real-time betting streams to detect anomalous patterns indicative of match-fixing, insider trading, or acute harm escalation during live events.
5. **Behavioral and cognitive science findings have not been integrated into AI prediction models for sports betting.** The extensive literature on heuristics (Gigerenzer & Brighton, 2009; Gigerenzer & Goldstein, 2011), information avoidance (Golman et al., 2017), counterfactual thinking (Byrne, 2015), and negativity bias (Baumeister et al., 2001) has not been operationalized as features or constraints in ML models that predict betting behavior, market movements, or individual decision quality in sports betting contexts.
6. **No longitudinal studies examine how AI-driven betting tools affect gambler behavior over time.** The existing ML studies in this domain (Wilkens, 2021; Auer & Griffiths, 2019) are cross-sectional or short-term, leaving unknown whether reliance on AI prediction tools exacerbates problem gambling, creates false confidence, or genuinely improves decision quality over extended periods.
7. **Explainable AI (XAI) methods have not been applied to sports betting prediction models.** As ML models used for sports prediction grow more complex (deep learning, ensemble methods), there is no research on making their outputs interpretable to bettors, regulators, or operatorsâa gap that is critical given the financial and harm-related stakes involved.
8. **The relationship between AI prediction model performance and actual betting market efficiency remains unresolved.** Wilkens (2021) tests ML models against tennis betting markets, but the broader question of whether increasingly sophisticated AI models systematically improve upon market-implied probabilitiesâor whether markets rapidly absorb publicly available AI-generated signalsâlacks rigorous, multi-sport, multi-year investigation.
9. **Cross-cultural and cross-jurisdictional comparisons of AI adoption in sports betting are missing.** The gambling harm frameworks (Abbott et al., 2018; Wardle et al., 2024) emphasize international variation, but no study examines how AI tools, regulatory approaches, and bettor engagement with AI differ across jurisdictions with varying legal and cultural attitudes toward sports betting.
10. **The application of AI to detect and prevent sports betting-related harm among vulnerable populations (e.g., older adults, adolescents) is unstudied.** While Tse et al. (2012) systematically review gambling among older adults and the conceptual frameworks identify vulnerability factors, no research has developed or tested AI-based screening or early-warning systems tailored to at-risk demographic groups in sports betting specifically.
