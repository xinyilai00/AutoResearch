SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature reveals a fragmented landscape in which only a small subset of papers directly addresses the intersection of artificial intelligence and stock market prediction. The most directly relevant contribution is Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020), who performed a comprehensive comparative analysis of machine learning methods for empirical asset pricing, demonstrating that tree-based models and neural networks substantially outperform traditional regression-based strategies in measuring risk premiums. This work is situated within a broader intellectual tradition spanning the Capital Asset Pricing Model (Fama & French, 2004), the evolution from efficient markets theory to behavioral finance (Shiller, 2003), and foundational time series econometrics such as the Dickey-Fuller unit root test (1979). Together, these works establish the theoretical and methodological baseline against which AI-driven predictors must be evaluated. Makridakis, Spiliotis, and Assimakopoulos (2018) further contribute by empirically comparing statistical and machine learning forecasting methods across a large corpus of time series, finding that ML methods do not uniformly dominate classical approachesâa cautionary result with direct implications for stock market forecasting.

A second major theme concerns the epistemological distinction between explanation and prediction. Shmueli (2010) and Shmueli and Koppius (2011) argue forcefully that explanatory power and predictive power are fundamentally different objectives, and that conflating them leads to methodological confusion. This distinction is highly relevant to AI predictors in stock markets, where practitioners often deploy black-box models optimized for prediction without regard for causal explanation, yet regulators and investors increasingly demand interpretability. On the methodological side, the literature provides extensive surveys of deep learning architectures (Alzubaidi et al., 2021; Janiesch et al., 2021), ensemble learning techniques (Mienye & Sun, 2022), decision tree classifiers (Charbuty & Abdulazeez, 2021), feature selection methods (Chen et al., 2020), and deep reinforcement learning (FranÃ§ois-Lavet et al., 2018)âall of which represent candidate techniques for stock market prediction but are largely discussed in general or non-financial contexts.

A third theme involves alternative data and sentiment signals. O'Connor, Balasubramanyan, and Routledge (2010) demonstrated that Twitter sentiment correlates with consumer confidence and political opinion polls at levels as high as 80%, establishing a precedent for using social media text as a predictive signal. The remaining papers address AI applications in domains such as education, healthcare, climate change, marketing, wildfire management, and NFT markets, providing methodological inspiration but no direct evidence regarding stock market prediction.
