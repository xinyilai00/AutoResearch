SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature broadly addresses the relationship between dietary sugar consumption and dental health, with particular emphasis on sugar-sweetened beverages and early childhood caries (ECC). Several studies establish that cariogenic dietary patterns — especially frequent exposure to sugary liquids and snacks — are a primary driver of dental caries in pediatric populations (Achalu et al., 2020; Inchingolo et al., 2023; Opinya, 2018). Research on media exposure further demonstrates that children are heavily targeted by advertising for confectionery and cariogenic products, which shapes consumption behaviors from an early age (Morgan et al., 2008). Intervention studies have focused on behavioral programs delivered by community health workers (Lumsden et al., 2023), policy-level measures such as soda taxation (Somji et al., 2016), and clinical approaches including fluoride intake assessment (Mir et al., 2020) and vitamin D status evaluation (Hofilena, 2015). Historical and cross-cultural evidence confirms that sugar-rich diets have been associated with caries formation across diverse populations and time periods (Fuß et al., 2018; Triambelas, 2014; Soares et al., 2021).

A second cluster of work examines the social determinants and structural factors that shape oral health outcomes in children and adolescents. Food insecurity, socioeconomic marginalization, and limited access to dental care compound the effects of poor diet, producing disparities in caries prevalence among vulnerable groups including Indigenous communities, migrant farmworkers, and populations in low-resource settings (Soares et al., 2021; Serna, 2014; Shomuyiwa & Bridge, 2023; Achalu et al., 2020). The oral microbiome has also received growing attention, with methodological guidance now available for designing rigorous clinical studies of microbial community shifts in response to dietary exposures (Zaura et al., 2020), and emerging evidence on natural antimicrobial agents such as honey that may modulate oral biofilms (Silva et al., 2022). Additionally, the digital information landscape surrounding ECC has been characterized, revealing widespread but uneven quality of online resources available to parents (Aguirre, 2022).

Despite this body of work, the literature is almost entirely oriented toward chronic, habitual dietary patterns — daily soda consumption, routine snacking habits, and long-term sugar exposure — rather than acute, episodic sugar consumption events. No study in the retrieved set specifically examines seasonal or holiday-associated candy consumption, such as Halloween, and its short-term or cumulative effects on children's dental health. The existing evidence base treats sugar intake as a relatively stable behavioral variable, leaving unexplored the question of whether concentrated, time-limited surges in candy consumption produce distinct cariogenic effects, acute shifts in oral microbiome composition, or differential outcomes depending on post-consumption oral hygiene practices.

GAPS:
1. No study has specifically investigated the impact of Halloween candy consumption — or any analogous seasonal, holiday-associated surge in candy intake — on children's dental health outcomes, including caries incidence, enamel demineralization, or acute changes in oral microbiota.
2. The literature has not examined the temporal dynamics of episodic high-sugar exposure events (e.g., consuming large quantities of candy over a period of days or weeks) versus chronic daily sugar intake, leaving unclear whether the cariogenic risk profile differs between these two consumption patterns.
3. There is no research on the moderating role of post-consumption oral hygiene behaviors (e.g., timing of brushing, rinsing, or water consumption) specifically following concentrated candy-eating episodes, despite evidence that hygiene practices interact with dietary sugar to shape caries risk (Opinya, 2018; Lumsden et al., 2023).
4. The types of candy most commonly distributed during Halloween (e.g., hard candies, gummies, chocolate, sour candies) have not been differentially assessed for their cariogenic potential in pediatric populations, even though the erosive and cariogenic properties of different sugar formulations and textures are likely to vary, as suggested by work on carbonated beverages and enamel damage (Inchingolo et al., 2023).
5. No study has used oral microbiome sequencing methods — despite established protocols for such work (Zaura et al., 2020) — to track acute shifts in microbial community composition before, during, and after a period of concentrated candy consumption in children.
6. The intersection of holiday candy consumption with existing socioeconomic disparities in oral health (Soares et al., 2021; Shomuyiwa & Bridge, 2023; Achalu et al., 2020) remains entirely unexplored; it is unknown whether children from food-insecure or low-income households experience differential dental health impacts from seasonal candy surges.
7. There is a gap in research on parental mediation strategies during Halloween and similar events — including candy rationing, substitution, or buyback programs — and whether such strategies have measurable effects on children's dental outcomes, despite evidence that parental involvement is a key factor in ECC prevention (Lumsden et al., 2023; Aguirre, 2022).
8. No longitudinal study has tracked whether a single high-exposure candy event (such as Halloween) produces lasting effects on caries progression or oral health behaviors, or whether its impact is fully transient and mediated by subsequent dietary and hygiene patterns.
