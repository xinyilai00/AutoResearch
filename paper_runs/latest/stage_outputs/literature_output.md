SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature on AI in stocks reveals a body of work concentrated along three principal axes. The first axis concerns the application of specific deep learning architectures to stock price forecasting. Papers such as Bao et al. (2017) combine wavelet transforms, stacked autoencoders, and LSTM networks for hierarchical feature extraction from financial time series, while Lu et al. (2020) propose a CNN-LSTM hybrid that leverages convolutional feature extraction alongside temporal memory mechanisms. Adebiyi et al. (2014) provide an early comparative benchmark demonstrating the superiority of artificial neural networks over ARIMA models for stock price prediction using NYSE data. Shah et al. (2019) offer a taxonomy of prediction techniques, acknowledging the fundamental tension between the random walk hypothesis and the potential of machine learning to extract short-term predictive signals. Gu et al. (2020) extend the scope beyond price prediction to empirical asset pricing more broadly, demonstrating that tree-based and neural network methods can substantially outperform traditional regression-based strategies in measuring asset risk premiums.

The second axis encompasses the broader methodological and theoretical foundations upon which financial AI research rests. Comprehensive surveys of deep learning (Alzubaidi et al., 2021; Janiesch et al., 2021), ensemble learning (Mienye & Sun, 2022), and machine learning for big data (Qiu et al., 2016; Nguyen et al., 2019) establish the technical toolkit available to researchers. Makridakis et al. (2018) raise important cautionary notes, finding that ML methods do not consistently outperform statistical approaches in time series forecasting across all horizons, a finding that complicates the narrative of ML superiority in finance. Zhou et al. (2021) introduce the Informer architecture for long sequence time-series forecasting, representing a class of Transformer-based models that have not yet been widely adopted in the stock prediction literature represented here. Shiller (2003) provides the theoretical backdrop from the finance side, tracing the evolution from efficient markets theory to behavioral finance and establishing the intellectual tension that motivates much of the empirical work on whether markets can be predicted.

The third axis addresses the governance, trustworthiness, and societal implications of AI systems. Work on AI accountability (Raji et al., 2020), transparency by design (Felzmann et al., 2020), and trustworthy AI (Díaz-Rodríguez et al., 2023) establishes frameworks for responsible AI deployment. However, these governance-oriented papers are almost entirely disconnected from the financial prediction literature, addressing AI ethics in general or in domains such as healthcare and education rather than in the specific high-stakes context of financial markets where algorithmic decisions carry direct economic consequences.

GAPS:
1. **Transformer architectures remain largely unexplored for stock prediction.** While the Informer (Zhou et al., 2021) and related Transformer variants have demonstrated strong performance in general long-sequence time-series forecasting, none of the stock-specific papers in this corpus apply attention-based Transformer models to equity price prediction, representing a significant architectural gap given the dominance of Transformers in adjacent sequence modeling domains.

2. **No integration of behavioral finance theory with modern deep learning approaches.** Shiller (2003) outlines the rich theoretical framework of behavioral finance — including investor sentiment, overreaction, and herding — yet none of the ML/DL stock prediction papers incorporate behavioral variables, sentiment indicators, or psychologically motivated features into their models, leaving a disconnect between financial theory and computational practice.

3. **Explainability and interpretability of AI models in financial contexts are entirely absent.** The corpus includes substantial work on trustworthy AI (Díaz-Rodríguez et al., 2023), transparency by design (Felzmann et al., 2020), and AI accountability (Raji et al., 2020), but none of these frameworks are applied to or evaluated within stock market prediction systems, despite the regulatory and fiduciary demands for explainability in financial decision-making.

4. **Ensemble methods are surveyed in general but not systematically applied to stock prediction.** Mienye and Sun (2022) provide a comprehensive survey of bagging, boosting, and stacking techniques, yet no paper in this corpus investigates ensemble combinations of heterogeneous deep learning architectures (e.g., combining CNN-LSTM with Transformer predictions) specifically for equity forecasting.

5. **The tension between efficient markets theory and ML predictive capability lacks rigorous modern examination.** Shah et al. (2019) mention the random walk hypothesis in passing, and Shiller (2003) predates the deep learning revolution, but no paper directly tests whether modern DL architectures can extract persistent alpha in ways that challenge or refine the efficient markets hypothesis using contemporary data and methodology.

6. **Alternative and unstructured data sources are not represented.** None of the retrieved papers incorporate social media sentiment, news text, satellite imagery, earnings call transcripts, or other unstructured alternative data into stock prediction models, despite the growing availability of such data and its demonstrated value in practitioner settings.

7. **Real-time, online, and continual learning paradigms are absent.** All stock prediction papers in this corpus employ offline training on historical data with static model architectures, with no investigation of online learning, continual adaptation, or concept drift detection mechanisms that would be critical for models deployed in non-stationary financial environments.

8. **Cross-market, multi-asset, and transfer learning approaches are unexplored.** Each stock prediction paper focuses on a single market or index, with no work examining whether models trained on one market can transfer knowledge to another, or whether multi-task learning across asset classes (equities, bonds, commodities) could improve prediction performance.

9. **Model robustness, adversarial vulnerability, and stress testing of financial AI models are unstudied.** While Makridakis et al. (2018) evaluate accuracy across forecasting horizons, no paper examines how stock prediction models perform under regime changes, market crashes, or adversarial perturbation — a critical gap given the tail-risk nature of financial markets.

10. **Feature selection methods are studied for general classification but not optimized for financial time series.** Chen et al. (2020) demonstrate feature selection for standard datasets, but no paper systematically investigates which technical indicators, fundamental ratios, or macroeconomic variables are most informative when combined with deep learning architectures for stock prediction, leaving architecture and feature engineering largely decoupled.
