SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature reveals several interconnected research streams relevant to the topic of classical music and attention span. A substantial body of work has examined music's broader effects on cognitive function, with studies demonstrating that music listening can enhance cognitive recovery after stroke (Särkämö et al., 2008), improve processing speed and memory in older adults when used as background music (Bottiroli et al., 2014), and influence learning outcomes through arousal-mood mechanisms (Lehmann & Seufert, 2017). Complementing these behavioral findings, neuroimaging and theoretical work has mapped the neural substrates of music perception, revealing that music listening activates widespread bilateral networks involved in attention, semantic processing, memory, and emotional processing (Koelsch, 2011; Binder et al., 2009). The attention system itself has been well characterized through studies of attentional blink phenomena (Olivers & Meeter, 2008; Olivers & Nieuwenhuis, 2006) and attentional modulation of other cognitive processes (Bantick et al., 2002), yet these paradigms have rarely been applied to music listening contexts.

A second major theme concerns the cognitive advantages conferred by musical training. Musicians have been shown to exhibit enhanced auditory working memory and attentional control compared to non-musicians (Pallesen et al., 2010), and musical training has been linked to improvements in cognitive function, mood, and quality of life in older adults (Seinfeld et al., 2013). Bidirectional transfer effects between music and language domains have also been documented, with tone language speakers and musicians sharing enhanced perceptual and cognitive abilities for musical pitch (Bidelman et al., 2013). These findings suggest that sustained engagement with music can produce domain-general cognitive benefits, though the specific contribution of classical music—as opposed to other genres or musical training broadly—remains largely unexamined.

A third prominent strand of research addresses music-based therapeutic interventions, particularly for dementia (van der Steen et al., 2018; Sung et al., 2011) and stroke rehabilitation (Särkämö et al., 2008), as well as music's role in psychological well-being more broadly (Croom, 2014). Additionally, the emotional and aesthetic dimensions of music experience have received considerable theoretical and empirical attention, with debates about which emotions music can induce (Scherer, 2004; Juslin, 2013), how aesthetic experiences unfold neurally (Brattico et al., 2013), and the neurohormonal mechanisms underlying music's social bonding effects (Tarr et al., 2014). Despite this rich landscape, the intersection of classical music specifically and sustained attention remains strikingly underdeveloped.

GAPS:
1. No study in the retrieved set specifically isolates classical music as the independent variable and sustained attention span as the dependent variable. Existing work examines music broadly, background music of various types, or active musical training, but the specific effect of listening to classical music on attentional capacity and duration has not been directly investigated with controlled experimental designs.
2. The distinction between passive classical music listening (as a background or pre-task intervention) and active classical music performance or training has not been systematically compared in relation to attention outcomes. Studies either examine musicians versus non-musicians or use generic background music, leaving the dose-response relationship and mode of engagement with classical music unexplored.
3. Sub-genre differences within classical music (e.g., Baroque, Classical, Romantic, Contemporary/Minimalist) have not been investigated for their differential effects on attention. Given that these sub-genres vary substantially in tempo, harmonic complexity, dynamic range, and rhythmic structure, the assumption that "classical music" is a uniform stimulus represents a significant unexamined variable.
4. The mechanistic pathway by which classical music might influence attention—whether through arousal modulation, mood enhancement, dopaminergic reward activation, entrainment of neural oscillations, or reduction of mind-wandering—has not been empirically tested in an integrated model. Individual papers address these mechanisms in isolation (e.g., dopamine and plasticity in Speranza et al., 2021; predictive coding in Vuust & Witek, 2014; arousal-mood in Lehmann & Seufert, 2017), but no study has combined them to explain classical music's specific attentional effects.
5. The literature is heavily skewed toward Western adult and elderly populations, with virtually no studies examining the effect of classical music on attention span in children, adolescents, or young-to-middle-aged adults in controlled settings. Cross-cultural work in music cognition (Jacoby et al., 2020) explicitly acknowledges this Western bias but does not address attention outcomes.
6. Longitudinal and causal evidence is absent. Existing studies on music and cognition are predominantly cross-sectional (e.g., comparing musicians to non-musicians) or employ very short-term interventions. Whether repeated exposure to classical music produces lasting improvements in attentional capacity, as opposed to transient state-level effects, has not been established through randomized controlled trials with adequate follow-up periods.
7. Individual difference variables—such as baseline attentional capacity, musical sophistication, personality traits (e.g., openness to experience), ADHD or attention-deficit status, and working memory capacity—have not been systematically examined as moderators of classical music's effect on attention. The one study addressing working memory capacity as a moderator (Lehmann & Seufert, 2017) used generic background music, not classical music specifically.
8. Ecologically valid attention measures are lacking. Most studies use brief laboratory-based attention tasks (e.g., attentional blink paradigms, Stroop tasks, processing speed tests), but no study has examined whether classical music affects sustained, real-world attentional behaviors such as reading comprehension duration, lecture engagement, or prolonged task persistence in naturalistic settings.
9. Contradictory findings exist regarding whether background music facilitates or impairs cognitive performance, with some studies showing benefits (Bottiroli et al., 2014) and others showing null or negative effects depending on task type and working memory capacity (Lehmann & Seufert, 2017). This contradiction has not been resolved specifically for classical music, which may have different cognitive load characteristics than the pop or ambient music used in most background music studies.
10. The neurophysiological correlates of attention during classical music listening have not been studied using modern neuroimaging or electrophysiological methods (e.g., EEG theta/beta ratios, fMRI attention network activation, pupillometry). While the neural basis of music perception has been reviewed (Koelsch, 2011) and attention networks have been mapped independently (Bantick et al., 2002; Binder et al., 2009), the intersection—how classical music modulates attention-related neural activity in real time—remains entirely uninvestigated.
