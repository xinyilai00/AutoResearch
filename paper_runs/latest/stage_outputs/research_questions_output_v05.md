CANDIDATE RESEARCH QUESTIONS:
1. Does the rigidity of fixed daily scheduling in standard 9-to-5 employment predict depressive and anxiety symptom severity independently of total weekly work hours, when controlling for occupation, income, and baseline health? | Gap addressed: Gap 2 — the granular structural features of conventional work schedules (fixed start/end times, scheduling rigidity) have not been isolated as independent variables affecting mental health. | Feasibility: Publicly available panel datasets such as the UK Household Longitudinal Study (Understanding Society) and the US National Health Interview Survey contain detailed work schedule variables alongside validated mental health instruments (e.g., GHQ-12, SF-12 MCS), enabling multivariate regression and propensity score matching without primary data collection.

2. To what extent does circadian misalignment — operationalized as the absolute difference between chronotype-derived preferred sleep-wake timing and mandated 9-to-5 work hours — predict clinically significant psychological distress among full-time office workers? | Gap addressed: Gap 3 — no retrieved study examines the interaction between circadian preference and standard 9-to-5 scheduling on mental health outcomes in the general working population. | Feasibility: The UK Biobank (publicly accessible to researchers) contains both chronotype assessments (Morningness-Eveningness questionnaire items) and mental health measures (PHQ-4, GAD-7, neuroticism scales) for over 500,000 participants, including employed subgroups with recorded work hours, enabling large-scale computationally reproducible analysis.

3. Does age cohort moderate the association between standard 9-to-5 work schedule exposure and mental health outcomes, such that younger workers (18-30) experience greater adverse effects from schedule rigidity than mid-career (31-50) or older workers (51-65)? | Gap addressed: Gap 5 — the literature treats working-age adults as a homogeneous group with respect to schedule effects, with no examination of age-cohort differences in how conventional employment affects mental health. | Feasibility: Long-running panel studies (PSID, HRS, Understanding Society) contain decades of age-stratified employment and mental health data; age-cohort interaction effects can be tested using hierarchical linear modeling and fixed-effects regression entirely through computational analysis of public-use microdata.

4. Do the fixed temporal boundaries inherent in 9-to-5 work schedules facilitate or hinder psychological detachment from work during non-work hours, compared to self-selected flexible schedules, as measured by work-related rumination and off-job recovery scores? | Gap addressed: Gap 4 — the literature on psychological detachment does not resolve whether the fixed structure of a 9-to-5 schedule is protective (clear boundaries) or harmful (imposed rigidity), representing an unresolved empirical contradiction. | Feasibility: The American Time Use Survey (ATUS) Well-Being Module and the European Working Conditions Survey (EWCS) contain publicly available data on work schedule type, off-job time use, and subjective well-being during specific daily activities, enabling comparative analysis of detachment indicators across schedule categories using existing microdata.

5. Does exposure to standard 9-to-5 work schedules differentially predict psychological distress for men versus women, and is this effect mediated by the volume of unpaid caregiving and domestic labor performed outside work hours? | Gap addressed: Gap 6 — the gender dimension of 9-to-5 work is underexplored; no retrieved study examines whether the standard schedule disproportionately burdens one gender through its interaction with caregiving and domestic labor expectations. | Feasibility: Time-use diary datasets (ATUS, Multinational Time Use Study) linked with mental health measures provide sex-disaggregated data on paid work hours, unpaid labor minutes, and well-being indicators; structural equation modeling and causal mediation analysis can be performed computationally on these public datasets.

6. How does the association between standard work schedule regularity and population-level psychological distress differ between countries with rigid work-hour norms and strong labor protections (e.g., Japan, South Korea) versus countries with flexible scheduling norms and shorter statutory hours (e.g., Denmark, Netherlands)? | Gap addressed: Gap 7 — the literature is concentrated in Western samples, and the mental health effects of 9-to-5 work in different cultural and regulatory contexts are almost entirely absent. | Feasibility: Cross-national datasets from the OECD Better Life Index, European Social Survey, World Values Survey, and WHO World Mental Health Surveys provide harmonized measures of work conditions and mental health across 30+ countries, enabling multilevel modeling with country-level institutional variables as moderators using publicly available data.

7. Over a 15-to-20-year employment span, does sustained exposure to standard 9-to-5 scheduling predict an accelerating, plateauing, or U-shaped trajectory in depressive symptoms, and does this trajectory differ from that observed among workers in non-standard or flexible schedules? | Gap addressed: Gap 10 — the cumulative, long-term trajectory of mental health under sustained 9-to-5 employment over decades has not been studied, leaving open whether conventional schedules carry hidden long-run psychological costs or benefits. | Feasibility: The Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID, 1968-present) and the Health and Retirement Study (HRS, 1992-present) provide publicly available longitudinal microdata spanning multiple decades with repeated measures of employment characteristics and mental health (CES-D, life satisfaction), enabling growth curve modeling and latent trajectory analysis computationally.

8. What are the within-workday temporal patterns of mood, fatigue, and perceived stress across a standard 9-to-5 day, and do specific structural features — including time since wake, proximity to scheduled breaks, and meeting density — predict momentary affective states? | Gap addressed: Gap 8 — no retrieved paper employs experience sampling or ecological momentary assessment to capture micro-temporal dynamics of mental health states as they unfold across the standard workday. | Feasibility: Several publicly available EMA/ESM datasets exist (e.g., the Experience Sampling Method datasets on OSF and ICPSR, the Day Reconstruction Method data from ATUS) that include timestamped affect ratings from employed participants; time-series analysis, multilevel modeling, and breakpoint detection can be applied computationally to characterize within-day affective dynamics without new data collection.

9. What is the dose-response relationship between daily sedentary screen time and meeting-hour exposure during standard 9-to-5 desk work and the prevalence of depressive symptoms, after adjusting for physical activity levels and occupational category? | Gap addressed: Gap 9 — no evaluated interventions or observational studies specifically target the unique stressors of sedentary desk work, prolonged screen time, and meeting-heavy schedules that characterize the most common work environment. | Feasibility: The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the Canadian Community Health Survey contain linked data on occupational sitting time, screen exposure, physical activity (accelerometer-measured in NHANES subsamples), and validated depression screening scores (PHQ-9), enabling dose-response curve estimation and threshold analysis using public-use data files.

10. Can standard 9-to-5 full-time employment be empirically distinguished from other employment categories (shift work, gig work, part-time, self-employment) as a distinct latent class with a unique mental health risk profile, using data-driven clustering of work schedule features and psychological outcomes? | Gap addressed: Gap 1 — standard 9-to-5 office employment has not been studied as its own distinct category of interest; the literature lacks a data-driven characterization of this modal work arrangement as a separate exposure class. | Feasibility: Large-scale occupational health surveys (e.g., EWCS, US National Survey of Occupational Safety and Health) contain multidimensional work characteristic variables (schedule regularity, autonomy, hours, location, task variety) and mental health outcomes for tens of thousands of workers; latent class analysis and cluster analysis can be applied computationally to identify whether 9-to-5 work forms a statistically distinct class with a distinguishable mental health signature.
