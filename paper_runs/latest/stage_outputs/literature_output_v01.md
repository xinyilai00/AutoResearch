SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The literature on AI in stocks spans several interconnected domains. The most prominent thread concerns the application of machine learning and deep learning methods to stock price prediction and asset pricing. Foundational work by (Gu et al., 2020) demonstrated that tree-based models and neural networks substantially outperform traditional regression-based approaches in measuring asset risk premiums, while (Shah et al., 2019) provided a comprehensive taxonomy of prediction techniques ranging from statistical methods to advanced computational approaches. Subsequent studies have proposed increasingly sophisticated architectures, including LSTM-CNN feature fusion models (Kim & Kim, 2019), hybrid ARFIMA-LSTM frameworks incorporating fractional-order derivatives (Bukhari et al., 2020), and graph-based CNN-LSTM models with leading indicators (Wu et al., 2021). (Nabipour et al., 2020) and (Hu et al., 2021) further confirmed the superiority of deep learning over shallow methods across different market contexts. These studies collectively establish that nonlinear models capture complex market dynamics more effectively than linear alternatives, though the magnitude of improvement varies considerably across datasets and evaluation criteria.

A second major theme involves the integration of sentiment analysis and natural language processing into financial prediction systems. (Sohangir et al., 2018) explored deep learning approaches for financial sentiment analysis using big data, while (Mishev et al., 2020) systematically evaluated sentiment extraction methods from traditional lexicons to transformer-based models, finding that modern NLP architectures improve sentiment classification accuracy for financial news. These efforts connect to the broader efficient market hypothesis debate (Shiller, 2003), as sentiment-driven price movements suggest that new information is not always instantaneously or rationally absorbed. Complementing this, (Chaboud et al., 2014) examined algorithmic trading in the foreign exchange market, demonstrating that machine-driven trading improves price efficiency by reducing arbitrage opportunities and return autocorrelation, which raises questions about the residual alpha available to AI-based stock trading strategies.

A third cluster of work provides broader context on AI's transformative potential across industries and its intersection with established financial theory. (Dwivedi et al., 2019) and (Vinuesa et al., 2020) offered multidisciplinary assessments of AI's societal impact, while (Huang & Rust, 2020) and (De Bruyn et al., 2020) developed strategic frameworks for AI adoption in marketing contexts. On the financial theory side, (Fama & French, 2004), (Harvey et al., 2015), and (Malmendier & Nagel, 2011) established the theoretical foundations of asset pricing, multiple-testing corrections, and behavioral influences on risk-taking—benchmarks against which AI-driven strategies must ultimately be evaluated. However, these foundational finance papers largely predate the modern deep learning era and do not directly engage with AI methodologies, creating a conceptual divide between the asset pricing theory literature and the AI prediction literature.

GAPS:
1. Most AI-based stock prediction studies evaluate models on a narrow set of markets—typically US equities or a single emerging market such as the Tehran Stock Exchange (Nabipour et al., 2020)—with virtually no systematic cross-market or cross-country comparative analysis examining how model performance varies across different market microstructures, regulatory environments, and levels of market efficiency.
2. The literature predominantly reports predictive accuracy using statistical metrics (RMSE, MAE, directional accuracy) rather than risk-adjusted trading profitability after accounting for transaction costs, slippage, and market impact, leaving a critical gap between reported model performance and real-world economic value (Gu et al., 2020; Kim & Kim, 2019; Wu et al., 2021).
3. There is no rigorous, controlled comparison between NLP/sentiment-driven prediction pipelines (Sohangir et al., 2018; Mishev et al., 2020) and pure time-series deep learning approaches (Nabipour et al., 2020; Bukhari et al., 2020) to determine under what conditions textual information provides incremental predictive power beyond price and volume data alone.
4. Explainability in AI-driven stock prediction remains almost entirely unaddressed; while (Bussmann et al., 2020) demonstrated Shapley-value-based interpretability for credit risk models, comparable explainable AI frameworks have not been developed or evaluated for stock market prediction, despite growing regulatory scrutiny of black-box trading algorithms.
5. The interaction between AI-driven trading strategies and different market regimes (bull, bear, high-volatility, low-volatility) is underexplored, as most studies evaluate models over fixed historical windows without analyzing temporal degradation, regime-conditional performance, or adaptive retraining strategies.
6. The vast majority of studies rely exclusively on traditional OHLCV (open, high, low, close, volume) data and standard macroeconomic indicators, with minimal investigation of alternative data sources—such as satellite imagery, credit card transactions, supply chain data, or ESG metrics—as inputs to deep learning stock prediction models.
7. There is a persistent disconnect between the modern AI/ML prediction literature and foundational asset pricing theory (Fama & French, 2004; Harvey et al., 2015); few studies evaluate whether AI-discovered predictive signals represent genuine risk factors, behavioral anomalies, or artifacts of data mining, and the multiple-testing corrections advocated by (Harvey et al., 2015) are rarely applied in the AI stock prediction literature.
8. Multi-asset and cross-asset AI prediction remains underexplored; most studies focus exclusively on equities, with limited research on how AI models perform when applied simultaneously to stocks, bonds, commodities, and cryptocurrencies within a unified portfolio optimization framework, despite (Fang et al., 2022) noting the growing inclusion of digital assets in institutional portfolios.
9. The behavioral and social dimensions of AI adoption in stock markets—such as how individual risk-taking experiences (Malmendier & Nagel, 2011) or social interactions and herding behavior (Manski, 2000) mediate the effectiveness of AI-assisted investment decisions—have not been studied, leaving a gap at the intersection of behavioral finance and AI-driven trading.
