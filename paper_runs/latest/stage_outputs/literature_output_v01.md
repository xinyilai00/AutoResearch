SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The literature on AI in the stock market can be organized around three interconnected strands. The first strand concerns the application of machine learning and deep learning methods to asset pricing and stock prediction. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) demonstrated that tree-based models and neural networks substantially outperform traditional regression-based approaches in measuring asset risk premiums, with economic gains that can double the performance of leading strategies. Selvin, Vinayakumar, and Gopalakrishnan (2017) explored LSTM, RNN, and CNN architectures with sliding window models for stock price prediction, while Nabipour, Nayyeri, and Jabani (2020) conducted a comparative analysis of machine learning and deep learning algorithms for trend prediction on the Tehran Stock Exchange. Shah, Isah, and Zulkernine (2019) provided a comprehensive taxonomy of prediction techniques, noting the persistent tension between random walk theory and the demonstrated predictive capacity of modern algorithms. Moody and Saffell (2001) introduced recurrent reinforcement learning as a framework for directly discovering investment policies without requiring separate forecasting models, framing trading as a stochastic control problem.

The second strand encompasses sentiment analysis as a potential input signal for financial models. Taboada (2015) established the linguistic foundations of sentiment analysis, Schouten and Frăsincar (2015) surveyed aspect-level approaches that could capture fine-grained opinions about specific companies or sectors, and Novak, Smailović, and Sluban (2015) mapped the sentiment landscape of emojis as a novel communication modality. Poria et al. (2017) extended sentiment analysis into multimodal contexts including video. However, these sentiment analysis advances have largely developed in parallel to, rather than in direct integration with, stock market prediction systems. The third strand provides the theoretical backdrop from finance: Shiller (2003) traced the evolution from efficient markets theory to behavioral finance, documenting the accumulation of anomalies that challenged the random walk hypothesis, while Hou, Chen, and Zhang (digesting anomalies) proposed a q-factor model to systematize the cross-section of average stock returns. Makridakis, Spiliotis, and Assimakopoulos (2018) raised an important methodological concern, finding that machine learning forecasting methods did not consistently outperform statistical methods across all time series, cautioning against uncritical adoption.

Beyond these core strands, several papers provide relevant context on AI capabilities and governance. Dwivedi et al. (2019) and Janiesch, Zschech, and Heinrich (2021) offered broad overviews of AI, machine learning, and deep learning paradigms. Ben-David et al. (2009) developed theoretical foundations for domain adaptation learning, which is highly relevant to the challenge of models trained on one market regime generalizing to another. Citron and Pasquale (2014) raised critical concerns about automated scoring and due process in algorithmic decision-making, while Salah, Rehman, and Nizamuddin (2019) explored blockchain as infrastructure for trustworthy AI systems. Acemoğlu and Restrepo (2018) examined the labor displacement and productivity effects of AI and automation.

GAPS:
1. No paper in the retrieved set applies large language models (LLMs) or foundation models to stock market prediction or financial analysis. Given the rapid emergence of models like GPT-4 and their demonstrated general-purpose NLP capabilities (Qin et al., 2023), there is a significant gap in understanding how LLMs can be leveraged for earnings call interpretation, financial news summarization, or zero-shot financial reasoning — and how their performance compares to purpose-built financial models.
2. Sentiment analysis research and stock market prediction research exist as largely disconnected bodies of work. No paper in this corpus demonstrates a rigorous, end-to-end pipeline that integrates modern aspect-level or multimodal sentiment analysis (e.g., from social media, video earnings calls, or emoji-laden retail investor discussions) with deep learning-based stock prediction models, leaving a clear integration gap.
3. There is no systematic study of model robustness and concept drift in AI-driven stock prediction across different market regimes (bull, bear, sideways, crisis). Ben-David et al. (2009) provide theoretical tools for domain adaptation, but these have not been applied to the financial domain where distributional shifts between market regimes are frequent and consequential.
4. Multimodal AI approaches — combining textual, visual, tabular, and time-series data simultaneously — are entirely absent from the stock market prediction literature in this corpus. While Flamingo (Alayrac et al., 2022) demonstrates the power of visual-language models for few-shot learning, no work has explored multimodal fusion architectures that jointly process financial charts, news text, satellite imagery, and structured financial data for market prediction.
5. The practical deployment challenges of AI trading systems are almost completely unaddressed. None of the reviewed papers systematically incorporate transaction costs, market microstructure effects, liquidity constraints, slippage, or market impact into their AI model evaluation frameworks, creating a substantial gap between reported backtesting performance and real-world deployability.
6. Transfer learning and reinforcement learning for adaptive portfolio management and dynamic position sizing remain underexplored. While Moody and Saffell (2001) introduced recurrent reinforcement learning for trading and Zhu et al. (2023) surveyed transfer learning in deep RL, no work combines these threads to create adaptive trading agents that can transfer learned policies across assets, sectors, or time periods.
7. Geographic and market-type diversity is severely lacking. The prediction studies focus predominantly on US or Chinese markets, with only Nabipour et al. (2020) examining an emerging market (Tehran). There is no comparative cross-market study examining whether AI prediction methods generalize across developed, emerging, and frontier markets with different microstructures and information environments.
8. The ethical, regulatory, and systemic risk implications of widespread AI adoption in stock markets are unexamined in this corpus. While Citron and Pasquale (2014) address algorithmic scoring in general and Salah et al. (2019) discuss blockchain for AI trustworthiness, no paper specifically investigates how large-scale deployment of AI trading systems might affect market stability, create herding behavior, or raise fairness concerns regarding information asymmetry between institutional and retail investors.
9. Explainability and interpretability of AI models in financial contexts represent a critical gap. As regulatory frameworks increasingly demand transparency in algorithmic decision-making (particularly in finance), none of the reviewed prediction papers incorporate explainable AI (XAI) methods to make their deep learning or ensemble model predictions interpretable to portfolio managers, regulators, or end investors.
10. The interaction between AI-driven trading and behavioral finance phenomena identified by Shiller (2003) remains unexplored. No study investigates whether AI models can systematically exploit documented behavioral biases (overreaction, disposition effect, herding) or whether AI-driven markets would attenuate these anomalies — a question at the intersection of the finance theory and AI prediction strands that neither body of work addresses.
