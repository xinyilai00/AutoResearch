SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature paints a broad picture of AI capabilities, limitations, and trustworthiness across multiple domains, but clusters around several interconnected themes. A substantial body of work examines the performance and failure modes of large language models, particularly in high-stakes domains such as medicine and healthcare. Papers such as Singhal et al. [3, 18] demonstrate that LLMs can encode clinical knowledge and approach expert-level medical question answering, yet they simultaneously acknowledge persistent challenges around hallucination, long-form reasoning, and real-world workflow integration. Complementing this, surveys by Gao et al. [21] and Bang et al. [22] directly confront the hallucination problem, proposing Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and multi-dimensional evaluation frameworks as partial remedies. A second major theme concerns the interpretability and transparency of machine learning systems more broadly. Carvalho et al. [4] survey methods for opening the "black box" of ML decision-making, while Dudley and Kristensson [30] explore interactive machine learning interfaces that allow human users to guide and inspect model behavior. These works establish that understanding *why* a model reaches a conclusion remains a significant open challenge. However, these applications largely treat AI as a tool for accelerating discovery rather than examining whether AI can critically evaluate the validity of its own outputs.

A third, smaller but important thread addresses the systemic issues in scientific practice itself. Nosek et al. Together, these works hint at the deep difficulty of self-error-detection but do not bridge the gap to artificial systems.

GAPS:
1. No study directly investigates whether AI systems can reliably detect that their own scientific conclusions are incorrect. The literature extensively documents that AI systems *make* errors (hallucinations, factual inaccuracies, reasoning failures), but the question of whether they can *self-identify* these errors post-hocâwithout external ground truth or human interventionâremains entirely unexamined as a focused research question.
2. There is no established framework or benchmark for evaluating AI metacognition in scientific reasoning contexts. While benchmarks exist for medical knowledge [3, 18], general reasoning and hallucination [22], and code vulnerability detection [25], none measure a model's ability to assess the reliability of its own scientific claims or to flag its own conclusions as potentially erroneous.
3. The relationship between AI confidence calibration and scientific error detection is underexplored. Existing work on hallucination [21, 22] focuses on whether outputs are factually correct relative to external references, but does not investigate whether a model's internal confidence signals (e.g., token-level probabilities, self-consistency across generations) can serve as reliable indicators that its own scientific conclusions are wrong.
4. There is a gap in understanding how domain-specific scientific reasoning (e.g., causal inference, experimental design evaluation, statistical validity assessment) interacts with AI self-correction capabilities. Papers on AI in chemistry [24], materials science [12, 23], and medicine [3, 18] show domain adaptation is possible, but none test whether models can recognize when their domain-specific scientific reasoning has led to invalid conclusions.
5. The literature on trustworthy AI [17, 19, 28] emphasizes external safeguardsâhuman oversight, regulatory frameworks, interpretability toolsâbut does not address the possibility or design of *intrinsic* AI self-verification mechanisms for scientific outputs. The assumption appears to be that error detection must come from outside the system, which may not be necessary if internal error-detection capabilities could be developed.
6. There is a contradiction between the predictive processing framework in cognitive science [1], which posits that biological brains are fundamentally error-detection and error-correction machines, and current AI architectures, which lack analogous self-monitoring mechanisms. This gap between biological and artificial error-detection has not been systematically explored as a design principle for improving AI scientific reliability.
7. No empirical work examines whether multi-agent or self-debate AI architectures (where one model instance critiques another's scientific conclusions) can serve as a proxy for genuine self-error-identification. While autonomous agent frameworks [11] and tool-augmented LLMs [24] exist, they have not been applied to the specific task of internal scientific quality control.
8. The impact of training data biases and scientific publication biases [8] on AI's ability to recognize erroneous conclusions has not been studied. If AI models are trained predominantly on published (and therefore potentially biased) scientific literature, their capacity to identify wrong conclusions may be systematically impaired in ways that mirror the human reproducibility crisisâbut this intersection remains unexplored.
