SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The literature on artificial intelligence in the medical field has expanded substantially over the past decade, with a significant body of work establishing the foundational role of deep learning and convolutional neural networks in medical imaging and diagnostics. Multiple reviews have documented the rapid progress of CNN-based approaches in radiology (Yamashita et al., 2018), medical image segmentation (Liu et al., 2021), and broader medical computer vision tasks (Esteva et al., 2021), while comprehensive surveys have mapped the overall landscape of AI applications across healthcare, encompassing both structured and unstructured data modalities (Jiang et al., 2017; Miotto et al., 2017; Bajwa et al., 2021). The availability of large-scale clinical datasets, most notably the MIMIC-III critical care database (Johnson et al., 2016), alongside growing volumes of electronic health records and biomedical big data (Dash et al., 2019), has served as a key enabler of this progress. At the same time, researchers have increasingly turned their attention to the translational gap between AI research and clinical deployment, identifying persistent challenges related to model explainability (Amann et al., 2020; Holzinger et al., 2019), algorithmic bias in EHR-derived models (Gianfrancesco et al., 2018), class imbalance in clinical datasets (Johnson & Khoshgoftaar, 2019), and the broader difficulties of delivering measurable clinical impact from AI systems (Kelly et al., 2019; Sutton et al., 2020).

A more recent and rapidly growing strand of literature focuses on large language models and foundation models in healthcare. Studies have evaluated the clinical knowledge encoded within LLMs using benchmarks such as MultiMedQA (Singhal et al., 2023), systematically reviewed the utility and limitations of ChatGPT in medical education, research, and practice (Sallam, 2023; Dave et al., 2023), and proposed the paradigm of generalist medical AI built on foundation models capable of performing diverse tasks with minimal task-specific labeled data (Moor et al., 2023). Concurrently, scholars have raised urgent calls for regulatory oversight of generative AI in clinical settings (Meskó & Topol, 2023) and have examined the ethical dimensions of LLM deployment in healthcare. The multidisciplinary nature of AI in healthcare has also been emphasized, with structured literature reviews highlighting the intersection of clinical, managerial, and policy perspectives (Secinaro et al., 2021; Dwivedi et al., 2019), and multimodal AI approaches have been proposed as a means to integrate heterogeneous biomedical data sources including imaging, genomics, EHRs, and wearable sensor data (Acosta et al., 2022).

Despite this breadth, the existing literature is heavily concentrated on technical feasibility studies, narrative and systematic reviews, and high-level conceptual discussions. The majority of empirical work focuses on a narrow set of clinical domains — particularly radiology, critical care, and diabetes management (Kavakiotis et al., 2017; ADA, 2021) — while many other medical specialties remain comparatively underexplored. Furthermore, the literature is predominantly authored from computer science and clinical perspectives, with limited engagement from health economists, implementation scientists, and patient representatives, raising questions about whether the current evidence base is sufficient to guide real-world adoption at scale.

GAPS:
1. There is a near-total absence of rigorous health economic evaluations examining the cost-effectiveness, return on investment, or budget impact of deploying AI systems in clinical workflows. While multiple papers discuss the transformative potential of AI in healthcare (Bajwa et al., 2021; Alowais et al., 2023), none provide empirical evidence on whether AI-driven interventions produce sufficient clinical benefit to justify their implementation and maintenance costs relative to existing standards of care.
2. Prospective, multi-center randomized controlled trials validating AI tools in real-world clinical settings are conspicuously scarce. The literature is dominated by retrospective studies using single-center datasets such as MIMIC-III (Johnson et al., 2016), and the gap between demonstrated algorithmic performance and proven clinical utility remains largely unbridged, as acknowledged by Kelly et al. (2019) but not systematically addressed with new empirical evidence since.
3. Longitudinal studies examining the long-term effects of AI-assisted clinical decision-making on patient outcomes, clinician behavior, and healthcare system dynamics are virtually nonexistent. Current research overwhelmingly focuses on cross-sectional or short-term predictive performance metrics rather than sustained impact over time.
4. The generalizability of AI models across diverse populations, healthcare systems, and geographic contexts remains severely underexplored. Most studies rely on data from large tertiary care hospitals in high-income countries, and there is minimal research on how models perform when transferred to low-resource settings, community hospitals, or populations with different demographic and epidemiological profiles.
5. Patient perspectives, experiences, and trust regarding AI-driven healthcare tools are almost entirely absent from the retrieved literature. While explainability is discussed from technical and clinician standpoints (Amann et al., 2020; Holzinger et al., 2019), there is no empirical work capturing how patients understand, accept, or are affected by AI involvement in their care decisions.
6. Clinical domains such as mental health, surgery, rehabilitation, geriatrics, and primary care are significantly underrepresented relative to radiology and critical care. The applicability of AI techniques to these specialties — which involve fundamentally different data types, decision-making processes, and patient interactions — has not been adequately investigated.
7. Privacy-preserving machine learning approaches, including federated learning and differential privacy, are not addressed in any of the retrieved papers, despite growing regulatory requirements for data protection in healthcare. The tension between the need for large-scale data aggregation (Dash et al., 2019) and patient privacy rights remains an unresolved and understudied challenge.
8. While multimodal AI has been proposed as a promising direction (Acosta et al., 2022; Moor et al., 2023), there is a lack of empirical studies that rigorously evaluate multimodal models against well-designed unimodal baselines across multiple clinical tasks, making it difficult to determine when and whether combining data modalities yields genuine performance gains versus added complexity.
9. The literature on LLMs in healthcare (Singhal et al., 2023; Sallam, 2023; Dave et al., 2023) is almost entirely composed of benchmark evaluations and narrative reviews, with no controlled studies examining the actual impact of LLM-assisted clinical documentation, patient communication, or diagnostic reasoning on workflow efficiency or clinical error rates in practice.
10. Standardized evaluation frameworks and reporting guidelines specific to AI applications in healthcare are lacking. The heterogeneity of metrics, datasets, and experimental designs across studies makes systematic comparison and meta-analysis extremely difficult, impeding cumulative scientific progress in the field.
