SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The literature on AI in the stock market spans three broad thematic clusters: foundational machine learning and deep learning methodologies, their direct application to financial prediction tasks, and the broader societal and institutional implications of deploying AI in financial contexts. On the methodological front, a substantial body of work establishes the theoretical and practical foundations of machine learning and deep learning techniques, including comprehensive treatments of neural network architectures, ensemble methods, deep reinforcement learning, and residual networks [5, 9, 11, 15, 16]. These methodological advances have been progressively adapted for financial time series analysis, with researchers demonstrating that deep learning frameworks—particularly those combining stacked autoencoders with long short-term memory networks—can extract hierarchical features from stock price data and yield superior forecasting performance compared to traditional statistical models such as ARIMA [13, 23]. The comparative study by Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) provides perhaps the most rigorous empirical benchmarking of machine learning methods for asset pricing, showing that tree-based models and neural networks substantially outperform classical regression-based strategies in measuring risk premiums [7].

A second major theme concerns the diversification of input data and trading strategies enabled by AI. Early work focused primarily on historical price and volume data, but more recent studies have incorporated alternative data sources, notably social media text, to improve stock movement prediction through deep generative models that jointly exploit textual and price signals [30]. The algorithmic trading literature has demonstrated that computer-driven trading activity improves price efficiency in foreign exchange markets by reducing arbitrage opportunities and return autocorrelation [21], while comprehensive surveys of stock prediction techniques catalog the proliferation of methods ranging from support vector machines to deep convolutional architectures [27, 28]. Adjacent domains such as cryptocurrency trading and NFT markets have also attracted scholarly attention, revealing unique market microstructures and behavioral patterns that differ meaningfully from traditional equity markets [22, 29]. Meanwhile, foundational work in financial economics—including the Dickey-Fuller unit root test and the Fama-French critique of the CAPM—continues to frame the theoretical boundaries within which AI-driven prediction must operate, reminding researchers that financial time series exhibit non-stationarity and that risk-return relationships are more complex than single-factor models suggest [1, 8].

A third thematic strand addresses the ethical, institutional, and macroeconomic dimensions of AI deployment in finance and beyond. Scholars have raised concerns about the opacity of automated scoring systems and the due process implications of algorithmic decision-making [24], while others have proposed frameworks for transparency by design in AI systems [26]. At the macroeconomic level, the productivity paradox—whereby AI advances coincide with stagnating productivity growth and wage inequality—raises questions about whether AI-driven gains in financial markets translate into broader economic benefits [18, 20]. The intersection of AI with financial inclusion has been explored in the context of digital fintech services aimed at underserved populations [25], and the potential of blockchain to complement AI through decentralized, trusted data sharing has been reviewed [14]. However, these broader considerations remain largely disconnected from the technical literature on stock market prediction, creating a notable schism between those who build AI trading models and those who study their societal consequences.

GAPS:
1. There is a pronounced absence of research on explainable AI (XAI) specifically tailored to stock market prediction. While general transparency frameworks exist [26], no study systematically addresses how to make deep learning-based stock forecasts interpretable to regulators, portfolio managers, and retail investors—a critical need given increasing regulatory scrutiny of algorithmic decision-making in finance.
2. The literature overwhelmingly focuses on offline backtesting of AI models, with virtually no rigorous studies examining real-time deployment performance, online learning adaptation, or model degradation under concept drift in live stock market environments. The gap between reported backtesting accuracy and actual trading performance remains largely unquantified.
3. Multi-modal data integration for stock prediction is severely underdeveloped. Only one study [30] combines text and price signals, and no work systematically integrates the growing universe of alternative data sources—satellite imagery, earnings call audio transcripts, SEC filing semantics, supply chain logistics data, and geolocation foot traffic—into unified deep learning architectures for equity valuation.
4. Federated learning, despite its demonstrated potential for privacy-preserving collaborative model training [2], has not been applied to stock market prediction. This represents a significant missed opportunity, as financial institutions could collaboratively improve predictive models without exposing proprietary trading data or client information.
5. Deep reinforcement learning for dynamic portfolio optimization in stock markets remains largely theoretical. While introductory surveys acknowledge its potential [9], there is a lack of empirical studies benchmarking deep RL agents against established portfolio strategies (e.g., mean-variance optimization, risk parity) over extended market cycles including stress periods.
6. Transfer learning across geographic markets and asset classes is almost entirely unexplored. Existing prediction models are typically trained and evaluated on single markets (e.g., NYSE, Tehran Stock Exchange), with no systematic investigation of whether representations learned from mature markets can be effectively transferred to emerging markets with different microstructures and regulatory environments.
7. The robustness and security of AI-driven stock prediction models against adversarial attacks, data poisoning, and market manipulation has received zero attention in the retrieved literature. Given that adversarial examples are well-documented in computer vision and NLP, the vulnerability of financial AI models to similar attacks—potentially enabling market manipulation—represents a critical and unstudied risk.
8. There is a fundamental disconnect between the technical AI prediction literature and traditional financial economic theory. While Gu et al. (2020) bridge this gap partially [7], no study embeds established financial theories—such as the efficient market hypothesis, behavioral finance biases, or no-arbitrage conditions—as structural constraints or inductive biases within deep learning architectures, potentially leading to models that learn spurious patterns inconsistent with economic fundamentals.
9. The systemic risk implications of widespread AI adoption in stock markets are unstudied. While algorithmic trading's microstructural effects have been examined in foreign exchange [21], no research investigates how the proliferation of similar AI trading strategies across market participants might amplify herding behavior, increase correlation during stress events, or contribute to flash crashes in equity markets.
10. The democratization of AI-powered stock market tools for retail investors remains an open gap. While financial inclusion through AI has been discussed in the context of digital banking [25], no study examines whether and how retail investors can effectively access, understand, and benefit from AI-driven stock market analytics, or whether AI tools exacerbate the informational advantage of institutional investors.
11. Regulatory frameworks specifically governing AI-driven stock trading and advisory are entirely absent from the literature. No paper addresses the question of how existing securities regulations (e.g., fiduciary duties, market manipulation statutes, disclosure requirements) should be adapted to accommodate AI systems that autonomously generate trading signals or execute trades, creating a significant policy vacuum.
12. Long-horizon stock prediction (multi-year investment horizons) using AI methods is dramatically understudied relative to short-term (daily or intraday) prediction. The literature provides no systematic comparison of how different AI architectures perform across varying investment time horizons, leaving a critical gap for long-term institutional investors such as pension funds and endowments.
