RELEVANT METHODOLOGIES:
- **Qualitative lyrical/thematic analysis**: Multiple studies employ close reading and thematic coding of hip-hop lyrics to examine race, masculinity, and identity. Heffernan (2016) conducts a thematic analysis of Kendrick Lamar's albums *Section.80*, *Good Kid, M.A.A.D City*, and *To Pimp a Butterfly*, examining how Lamar critiques institutionalized racism and articulates love as a mechanism for social change. Mouaoued (2025) similarly uses qualitative textual analysis to examine how Kendrick Lamar challenges racism, oppression, and white supremacy through hip-hop. Apol (2016, 2017) performs a literary-critical reading of Lamar's discography, situating his work within broader cultural and artistic posterity.
- **Multimodal analysis (lyrics + visual media)**: Lindmark (2019) extends lyrical analysis to music videos, interpreting the visual and lyrical rhetoric of Kendrick Lamar's "HUMBLE." alongside works by Childish Gambino and Beyoncé, arguing these artists have ushered in a new mode of sociopolitical commentary through multimedia hip-hop art.
- **Narrative analysis of masculinity and mental health in rap**: Hart (2019) traces the evolution of masculinity and mental health narratives across 30 years of rap music in Britain and America, using narrative analysis to document how artists have shifted from stoic hypermasculinity toward promoting healthier conversations around men's mental health and coping mechanisms.
- **In-depth interview-based audience reception study**: Erp et al. (2024) conduct semi-structured in-depth interviews (N = 20+) with hip-hop consumers to investigate how listeners give meaning to hip-hop as a genre, filling a gap in audience-centered hip-hop research. This is one of the few studies that centers listener interpretation rather than textual analysis alone.
- **Computational lyrical emotion and theme analysis**: Chowdary et al. (2024) use computational NLP methods to analyze the link between lyrical emotions, thematic content, and depression risk, demonstrating that specific lyrical themes and emotional valences can intensify negative states in listeners, particularly those with mood disorders. This provides a quantitative, scalable methodology for analyzing lyrical content.
- **Gendered performativity framework**: Miles (2020) applies a Black rural feminist framework to trap music, examining how gendered performativity is constructed and contested within hip-hop lyrics, treating lyrics not as literal representations but as sites of articulation.
- **Affective exchange and IP analysis**: Linscott (2025) analyzes the Drake-Kendrick Lamar beef through the lens of affective exchange, positing "love" as a form of currency within their conflict, and examines the legal and intellectual property dimensions of their rivalry.
- **Sociopolitical development framework for Black youth and media**: Anyiwo (2019) examines how racism and media intersect to shape the sociopolitical development of Black youth, including how Black youth engage in racial justice activism through both traditional and online action, providing a framework for understanding youth engagement with racialized media content.

RELEVANT DATASETS:
- **Kendrick Lamar's discography (Section.80, Good Kid M.A.A.D City, To Pimp a Butterfly, DAMN.)**: Used extensively by Heffernan (2016), Apol (2016, 2017), Mouaoued (2025), Lindmark (2019), and Linder (2018) as primary textual data for thematic and lyrical analysis. These albums serve as the core corpus for studying Lamar's treatment of race, spirituality, and identity.
- **Drake and Kendrick Lamar beef materials (2024)**: Linscott (2025) uses the public diss tracks, social media exchanges, and associated lawsuit documents between Drake and Kendrick Lamar as primary source material for analyzing affective exchange and IP claims.
- **Hip-hop listener interview data**: Erp et al. (2024) generated a qualitative interview dataset of hip-hop consumers (N = 20+), providing audience-reception data on how listeners interpret hip-hop lyrics and genre meaning.
- **Lyrical emotion datasets (computational)**: Chowdary et al. (2024) work with lyrical corpora annotated for emotional valence and thematic content, linked to depression risk indicators, demonstrating the feasibility of computational analysis of song lyrics at scale.
- **30-year corpus of rap music (Britain and America)**: Hart (2019) draws on a broad historical corpus of rap spanning three decades to trace narrative shifts in masculinity and mental health discourse.
- **No dataset of white teenage social media engagement with Drake/Kendrick content was identified in any of the provided papers.** This is a critical gap.

PRIOR QUANTITATIVE RESULTS:
- **Limited quantitative findings in the provided corpus**: The majority of the relevant papers are qualitative, interpretive, or humanistic in nature. Specific effect sizes, statistical tests, or large-scale quantitative results are largely absent from the provided literature.
- **Chowdary et al. (2024)** represent the most quantitatively oriented study, using NLP-based emotion classification and thematic modeling on lyrical corpora to establish links between lyrical emotional content and depression risk. While specific effect sizes are not detailed in the abstract, the study demonstrates that computational methods can reliably classify lyrical emotions and themes and correlate them with mental health outcomes.
- **Erp et al. (2024)** report qualitative findings from N = 20+ in-depth interviews, identifying thematic patterns in how hip-hop listeners construct meaning from the genre, though no quantitative metrics are provided.
- **Hart (2019)** documents a qualitative historical shift from hypermasculine stoicism to emotional vulnerability in rap over a 30-year period but does not provide quantitative measurements of this shift.
- **Palattella (2020)** examines emo rap and collective despair among American adolescents, documenting the convergence of hip-hop and emotional vulnerability themes, but the abstract does not report specific quantitative engagement metrics.
- **No study in the provided corpus reports quantitative social media engagement data (likes, shares, comment sentiment scores, subreddit activity metrics) specifically for white teenage users in relation to Drake or Kendrick Lamar content.**

WHAT THIS QUESTION STILL NEEDS:

The existing literature provides a strong qualitative foundation for understanding how Kendrick Lamar and, to a lesser extent, Drake engage with themes of race, masculinity, and emotional vulnerability in their lyrical and visual output. Scholars have thoroughly documented Lamar's critique of institutionalized racism (Heffernan, 2016; Mouaoued, 2025), his integration of spiritual and identity themes (Linder, 2018; Apol, 2017), and the broader evolution of masculinity and mental health narratives in rap (Hart, 2019; Palattella, 2020). The Drake-Kendrick Lamar conflict itself has been analyzed as an affective and legal exchange (Linscott, 2025). However, nearly all of this work is qualitative and artist-centered, relying on close reading, thematic coding, or interpretive frameworks applied to lyrics and music videos. There is a conspicuous absence of systematic, comparative quantitative analysis that directly codes and compares the lyrical content of Drake and Kendrick Lamar across the three thematic axes of race, masculinity, and emotional vulnerability using a replicable coding scheme or computational NLP pipeline.

More critically, the audience reception side of this research question is almost entirely unaddressed. While Erp et al. (2024) provide a valuable interview-based study of hip-hop listener meaning-making, and Anyiwo (2019) examines Black youth sociopolitical engagement with racialized media, no study in the retrieved corpus examines how white teenage users specifically engage with, interpret, or respond to the lyrical themes of Drake and Kendrick Lamar on social media platforms. There is no existing research that analyzes Reddit posts, Twitter/X threads, or other public social media data to measure sentiment, engagement patterns (upvotes, replies, shares), or thematic framing among self-identified white teenagers in relation to these two artists. This represents a fundamental gap: the question of how racially and emotionally charged hip-hop content is received by a demographic that is both outside the primary cultural origin of the genre and at a formative developmental stage remains entirely unanswered.

A new study must therefore accomplish three things. First, it must conduct a systematic comparative content analysis of Drake's and Kendrick Lamar's discographies, using either a validated coding scheme or computational NLP methods (building on the approach of Chowdary et al., 2024) to quantify thematic differences in race, masculinity, and emotional vulnerability. Second, it must collect and analyze public social media data from Reddit and Twitter/X, identifying self-identified white teenage users through demographic signals or self-disclosure, and measuring their engagement patterns (sentiment scores, interaction frequency, thematic framing) in response to content from both artists. Third, it must link the two analyses — correlating specific lyrical themes with specific patterns of audience engagement and sentiment — to determine whether and how the thematic differences between Drake and Kendrick Lamar correspond to measurably distinct reception patterns among this demographic. Without this integrated, mixed-methods approach combining computational lyrical analysis with social media audience analytics, the relationship between hip-hop thematic content and cross-demographic audience engagement will remain speculative.
