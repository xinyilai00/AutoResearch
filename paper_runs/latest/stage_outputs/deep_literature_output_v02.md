RELEVANT METHODOLOGIES:
- **Dynamic Structural Equation Modeling (DSEM) / Continuous-Time Modeling**: Marciano, Driver, and Schulz (2022) [28] employed dynamic modeling approaches on intensive longitudinal data (2,548 assessments over 45 consecutive days in 82 adolescents) to examine bidirectional, within-person relationships between smartphone use (measured via objective trace data) and well-being. This approach allowed separation of within-person from between-person effects and captured the temporal dynamics (ephemeral vs. sustained effects) of the smartphone use–well-being relationship, finding that effects were positive but short-lived.
- **Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) via Smartphones**: de Vries, Baselmans, and Bartels (2020) [19] systematically reviewed smartphone-based EMA studies of well-being, establishing that repeated within-day assessments via smartphones can capture momentary fluctuations in affect and behavior that traditional retrospective surveys miss. This methodology is critical for studying temporal dynamics because it provides the dense time-series data needed for cross-lagged or dynamic panel models.
- **Cross-Lagged Panel Modeling (CLPM) and Random-Intercept CLPM**: While none of the retrieved papers explicitly applied a traditional cross-lagged panel model to large-scale cohort data (e.g., ABCD Study or MCS), the methodological logic of examining bidirectional lagged effects is present in [28], which tested reciprocal effects between smartphone use and well-being at the within-person level. The broader CLPM/RI-CLPM framework—separating stable trait-like differences from time-varying within-person processes—is the methodological standard that the research question targets but which remains under-applied to publicly available longitudinal cohorts.
- **Smartphone Trace Data as Objective Usage Measurement**: Both [28] and [25] highlight the use of objectively logged smartphone app usage traces (screen time, app opens, notifications) rather than self-reported usage, which is known to be unreliable. Li et al. (2022) [25] reviewed datasets and methods for smartphone app usage analysis, documenting the types of fine-grained behavioral traces (session duration, app category, temporal patterns) that can serve as objective indicators of use intensity.
- **Systematic/Critical Review of Smartphones in Mental Health**: Bauer, Glenn, and Geddes (2020) [23] provided a critical review of smartphone applications in mental health care, identifying key challenges including data integration with electronic medical records, privacy concerns, and the gap between data collection and clinical utility—context essential for interpreting what smartphone-derived data can and cannot tell us about adolescent mental health.

RELEVANT DATASETS:
- **Intensive longitudinal smartphone trace dataset (Marciano et al.)**: The dataset in [28] comprises 2,548 well-being assessments paired with objective smartphone usage trace data collected over 45 consecutive days from 82 adolescents. This is directly relevant as it demonstrates the feasibility and analytic approach for studying within-person dynamics between phone use and well-being in adolescents, though the sample is small and not from a major public cohort.
- **Smartphone App Usage Datasets reviewed by Li et al. (2022) [25]**: This review catalogs various datasets used for smartphone app usage analysis, including app usage traces, session logs, and behavioral data that reveal connections between usage patterns and user characteristics. These datasets are relevant for understanding what objective usage indicators are available and how they have been operationalized.
- **ABCD Study and UK Millennium Cohort Study (MCS)**: These are the target datasets specified in the research question. None of the retrieved papers applied cross-lagged panel modeling to these specific cohorts for the smartphone–mental health question. The ABCD Study provides longitudinal neurodevelopmental and behavioral data on over 11,000 children, and the MCS follows approximately 19,000 children born in the UK in 2000–2001, both containing relevant variables on screen time/digital media use and mental health outcomes. Their absence from the retrieved literature represents a critical gap.

PRIOR QUANTITATIVE RESULTS:
- **Within-person effects of smartphone use on well-being are positive but ephemeral [28]**: Marciano et al. found that at the within-person level, increased smartphone use was associated with small, transient improvements in well-being rather than sustained harm. The effects dissipated quickly, suggesting that the temporal granularity of measurement matters enormously—daily or momentary assessments capture dynamics that longer-interval surveys (e.g., annual waves) may entirely miss or misrepresent.
- **Between-person vs. within-person dissociation [28]**: The study demonstrated that between-person associations (adolescents who use phones more overall report different well-being levels) do not generalize to within-person processes (when a given adolescent uses their phone more than usual, their well-being changes in a specific direction). This is a critical finding because most prior large-cohort studies have relied on between-person comparisons, potentially conflating selection effects with causal processes.
- **EMA captures fluctuations missed by retrospective measures [19]**: The systematic review by de Vries et al. established that smartphone-based EMA studies consistently find substantial within-day variability in well-being and behavior, with compliance rates generally adequate for longitudinal analysis, supporting the feasibility of intensive longitudinal designs in adolescent populations.
- **Objective trace data vs. self-report discrepancy [25, 28]**: Both papers underscore that self-reported smartphone use correlates poorly with objectively logged usage traces, with users systematically over- or under-estimating their use. This measurement issue has direct implications for effect size estimation: studies relying on self-report may produce biased cross-lagged coefficients.

WHAT THIS QUESTION STILL NEEDS:
The most significant gap is that no retrieved study has applied cross-lagged panel modeling—particularly the modern random-intercept CLPM (RI-CLPM) variant that separates stable between-person differences from within-person temporal dynamics—to large, publicly available longitudinal cohort datasets such as the ABCD Study or the UK Millennium Cohort Study for the specific question of smartphone use intensity and adolescent depressive and anxiety symptoms. The existing evidence from [28] is based on a small sample (N=82) with intensive measurement over a relatively short window (45 days), which is excellent for capturing micro-level temporal dynamics but cannot speak to developmental trajectories over months or years. Large cohort studies with annual or biennial waves spanning adolescence (e.g., ABCD with its planned 10-year follow-up, MCS with waves from age 7 through late adolescence) offer the statistical power and developmental timescale needed to determine whether the ephemeral within-person effects observed at the daily level accumulate into meaningful long-term associations with clinical-level depressive and anxiety symptomatology.

Methodologically, a new study must address several unresolved issues. First, it must employ the RI-CLPM or an equivalent model that properly disaggregates between-person confounds (e.g., pre-existing vulnerability to depression leading to more phone use) from genuine within-person temporal precedence (e.g., a surge in phone use at time t predicting increased depressive symptoms at time t+1, controlling for the person's own baseline). Second, it must reconcile the measurement mismatch between the coarse annual waves typical of large cohorts and the fine-grained daily dynamics documented in [28]—specifically, whether annual-lag cross-lagged effects are interpretable given that the true causal dynamics may operate on much shorter timescales. Third, it should use objective or at least multi-method assessments of smartphone use intensity, as the self-report bias documented in [19, 25, 28] threatens the validity of cross-lagged coefficients derived from survey-only data.

A new study must therefore: (1) obtain and harmonize longitudinal data from the ABCD Study and/or MCS, extracting validated measures of smartphone/screen use intensity and standardized depressive and anxiety symptom scales across at least three waves; (2) fit both traditional CLPM and RI-CLPM models to test whether the directionality of effects (phone use → symptoms vs. symptoms → phone use) holds after controlling for stable individual differences; (3) conduct sensitivity analyses examining whether effect directionality varies by age, sex, or baseline symptom severity; and (4) explicitly discuss the interpretive limitations imposed by wave spacing on claims about temporal dynamics, ideally triangulating with findings from intensive longitudinal designs like [28] to bridge the timescale gap. This would represent the first rigorous, large-scale test of directional and temporal hypotheses about smartphone use and adolescent internalizing symptoms using publicly available data.
