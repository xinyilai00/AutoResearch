RELEVANT METHODOLOGIES:
- **Automatic music genre classification using audio descriptors (Guaus, 2009)**: Developed a computational pipeline for extracting low-level and high-level audio features (spectral, temporal, rhythmic descriptors) from music signals and feeding them into classifiers, grounded in musicological and perceptual theories. This provides the foundational MIR (Music Information Retrieval) framework for building acoustic profiles of genre categories from raw audio.
- **Hierarchical MIDI-based genre classification with hybrid classifiers (McKay, 2004)**: Built a software system that classifies MIDI files into hierarchically organized taxonomies of musical genres using an extensive library of high-level musical features (harmonic, rhythmic, melodic, instrumental). The hybrid classification system combined hierarchical, flat, and round-robin strategies, demonstrating that sub-genre-level distinctions are computationally tractable when features are carefully engineered.
- **Computational tonality-based style analysis of classical music audio (Weiß, 2017)**: Directly addressed computational differentiation of classical music styles using tonality-based features extracted from audio recordings. Developed algorithms for harmonic and tonal feature extraction specifically designed for classical music, making this the most methodologically relevant work for distinguishing Baroque, Classical, Romantic, and contemporary styles on acoustic grounds.
- **Latent Profile Analysis for sub-genre taste differentiation (Siebrasse & Wald-Fuhrmann, 2023)**: Applied Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) to sub-genre liking patterns within classical music listeners (N = 2,086, with n = 1,749 who liked European classical music). Identified four to six distinct taste classes within each of five popular genres, demonstrating that meaningful sub-genre-level distinctions exist and can be statistically modeled. While focused on listener preferences rather than acoustic features, the LPA methodology is directly transferable to clustering acoustic profiles.
- **Entropy-based comparative music analysis (Febres & Jaffé, 2017)**: Analyzed polyphonic music files using a "Fundamental Scale" that produces Minimal Entropy Description, creating a novel representational space for comparing music pieces. Developed methods to adjust textual descriptions across scales of observation and to model structural properties. This information-theoretic approach offers an alternative to standard MIR features for characterizing complexity differences across classical sub-genres.
- **Emotion-to-Music Mapping Atlas / EMMA database construction (Strauß et al., 2024)**: Built a systematically organized online database of emotionally evocative music excerpts, categorized according to taxonomies derived from general emotion models (basic emotions, affective circumplex), rated for perceived emotions with interrater agreement metrics. This provides a methodological template for mapping acoustic features to arousal/valence dimensions relevant to therapeutic applications.
- **Emotion-based analysis and classification of audio music (Panda, 2019)**: Developed computational methods for classifying music based on emotional content rather than genre labels, bridging acoustic feature extraction with affective response models. Relevant for constructing emotion-driven rather than genre-driven taxonomies.
- **MIR-based sub-genre classification in video game music (Hwang et al., 2026)**: Extracted quantifiable musical features from VGM across identified game genres using systematic MIR approaches, demonstrating that sub-genre-level acoustic differentiation is feasible even in domains where formal musicological taxonomies are underdeveloped. The feature extraction pipeline (tempo, key, energy, spectral features) is directly applicable to classical sub-genre analysis.
- **Neuroaesthetic study of atonal vs. tonal music perception (Mencke et al., 2019)**: Investigated how atonal music (characterized by absence of tonal hierarchical organization, low consonance, no tonic reference) is processed compared to Western tonal music, using neuroaesthetic frameworks. Relevant for understanding how structural differences in harmonic organization (which vary systematically across Baroque through contemporary minimalism) affect cognitive engagement and emotional response.
- **Pitch class profile analysis and circular pitch representation (Purwins, 2005)**: Developed computational models of pitch perception using toroidal representations of the 24 major/minor keys, converging psychoacoustic experiments with geometric music theory and computer simulations. Provides methods for quantifying tonal/harmonic differences across classical sub-genres.

- **EMMA (Emotion-to-Music Mapping Atlas) database (Strauß et al., 2024)**: A systematically organized online database of emotionally evocative music excerpts with emotion ratings, interrater agreement values, and categorization according to affective circumplex and basic emotion models. Directly relevant as a source of annotated emotional music stimuli that could be extended or cross-referenced with classical sub-genre classifications.
- **MIDI genre classification corpus (McKay, 2004)**: A collection of MIDI files organized into hierarchical genre taxonomies, used for training and evaluating automatic classification systems. The hierarchical structure including classical sub-genres makes this a potential source for feature extraction from symbolic representations.

- **Sub-genre taste classes within classical music (Siebrasse & Wald-Fuhrmann, 2023)**: Latent Profile Analysis identified four to six distinct taste classes within each of five genres (including European classical music) in a sample of N = 2,086 (with n = 1,749 classical music likers). This demonstrates that sub-genre-level differentiation is statistically meaningful and that broad genre labels mask substantial within-genre heterogeneity.
- **Entropy-based music characterization (Febres & Jaffé, 2017)**: Demonstrated that the Minimal Entropy Description scale provides a quantifiable, comparable metric across music pieces, enabling placement of pieces in a novel representational space. Showed that entropy content differs measurably across musical styles, providing a potential acoustic dimension for distinguishing sub-genres by structural complexity.
- **Hybrid classification accuracy for hierarchical genre taxonomies (McKay, 2004)**: Achieved classification results using a hybrid system combining hierarchical, flat, and round-robin classification strategies on MIDI data with an extensive feature library, demonstrating that hierarchical sub-genre distinctions are computationally separable, though specific accuracy figures are not provided in the abstract.
- **Tonality-based style differentiation (Weiß, 2017)**: Developed and validated computational algorithms for extracting tonality-based features from classical music audio that can distinguish stylistic periods, though specific classification accuracies are not detailed in the abstract.

WHAT THIS QUESTION STILL NEEDS:

The retrieved literature reveals a substantial methodological toolkit for computational music analysis — including MIR feature extraction pipelines, hierarchical genre classification, tonality-based style analysis, entropy measures, and emotion-music mapping — but a critical gap exists at the intersection of these methods with the specific research question. No paper in this set directly addresses the acoustic profiling of classical music sub-genres (Baroque, Classical period, Romantic, contemporary minimalism) along dimensions specifically hypothesized to influence arousal regulation and cognitive engagement in dementia populations. The existing computational genre classification literature (Guaus, 2009; McKay, 2004; Weiß, 2017) focuses on genre identification as an end in itself, not on mapping acoustic features to therapeutic mechanisms. Similarly, the emotion-music mapping work (Strauß et al., 2024; Panda, 2019) addresses general emotional responses rather than the specific arousal regulation and cognitive engagement pathways relevant to dementia care. The sub-genre differentiation work by Siebrasse and Wald-Fuhrmann (2023) demonstrates that within-genre heterogeneity is real and measurable, but operates at the level of listener preference rather than acoustic feature analysis, and does not consider therapeutic outcomes.

A new study must accomplish several things that none of the existing papers achieve. First, it must construct a curated corpus of classical music recordings systematically sampled across the four target sub-genres (Baroque, Classical period, Romantic, contemporary minimalism), with sufficient representation to support statistical comparison. Second, it must extract a comprehensive set of acoustic features — drawing on the MIR descriptor libraries established by Guaus (2009), the tonality-based features of Weiß (2017), and the entropy measures of Febres and Jaffé (2017) — and then reduce these to dimensions specifically linked to arousal regulation (e.g., tempo variability, dynamic range, harmonic complexity, spectral roughness, rhythmic predictability) and cognitive engagement (e.g., tonal clarity, structural predictability, melodic contour complexity). Third, it must validate these dimension mappings against either psychophysiological measures (e.g., heart rate variability, EEG, skin conductance) or behavioral outcomes in dementia populations, which no existing paper in this set attempts. The neuroaesthetic work of Mencke et al. (2019) on tonal versus atonal processing provides a conceptual bridge, but has not been applied to the gradient of harmonic complexity spanning Baroque through contemporary minimalism, nor to clinical populations.

Finally, the construction of a computational taxonomy of therapeutic suitability requires integrating the acoustic profiling with clinical outcome data — a step that is entirely absent from the current literature. The Latent Profile Analysis approach of Siebrasse and Wald-Fuhrmann (2023) offers a promising statistical framework for identifying natural clusters of acoustic profiles, and the hierarchical classification strategies of McKay (2004) could be adapted to build a supervised taxonomy if therapeutic suitability labels were available. However, the fundamental missing piece is empirical data linking specific acoustic dimensions of classical sub-genres to measurable outcomes in dementia patients. A new study must therefore be interdisciplinary, combining MIR feature extraction, musicological period expertise, and clinical gerontology methods, and must produce both an open acoustic feature dataset and a validated predictive model mapping sub-genre acoustic profiles to therapeutic suitability scores. Without this empirical bridge, any computational taxonomy will remain theoretically motivated but clinically ungrounded.
