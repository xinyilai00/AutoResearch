SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature clusters around several interconnected themes relevant to computer usage and adolescent mental health. A substantial body of work establishes the epidemiological foundation: the Global Burden of Disease studies [1, 2] and the WHO World Mental Health Surveys [7, 11] document that mental disorders represent a leading cause of disability worldwide, with a large-scale meta-analysis by Solmi et al. [6] confirming that the peak age of onset for many mental disorders falls squarely within adolescence and early adulthood. This epidemiological backdrop has motivated research into modifiable risk factors, among which digital technology use has emerged as a prominent candidate. Systematic reviews by Keles et al. [16], Stiglic and Viner [23], and Odgers and Jensen [30] synthesize evidence linking screen time and social media use to depression, anxiety, and psychological distress in adolescents, though the effect sizes are generally small and the evidence is predominantly cross-sectional. Complementary work on sedentary behaviour [3, 12, 13] positions screen-based sitting as an independent risk factor for poor mental health outcomes in youth, even after controlling for physical activity levels, while research on sleep [18, 21] identifies smartphone use as a contributor to sleep disruption that in turn exacerbates depressive and anxious symptomatology.

A second major theme concerns the deployment of digital technologies as mental health interventions rather than risk factors. Randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews demonstrate that conversational agents delivering cognitive-behavioural therapy [9], mobile health applications [15, 24, 26], virtual reality environments [20], and gamified programmes [22] can produce measurable, albeit modest, improvements in mental health symptoms among young adults. However, engagement and sustained uptake remain persistent challenges [26]. The COVID-19 pandemic intensified both themes simultaneously: multiple studies document sharp increases in anxiety, depression, and psychological distress among adolescents and college students during lockdowns [5, 8, 19, 25, 28, 29], a period during which computer and screen use surged dramatically, thereby conflating pandemic-related stressors with digital exposure in ways that are difficult to disentangle.

Despite this breadth, the literature is characterized by methodological and conceptual limitations that constrain causal inference and practical guidance. The overwhelming reliance on cross-sectional designs and self-reported screen time, the tendency to treat "computer use" or "screen time" as monolithic constructs, and the scarcity of mechanistic or longitudinal research all leave fundamental questions unresolved about how, for whom, and under what conditions computer usage shapes adolescent mental health.

GAPS:
1. The vast majority of studies linking computer or screen use to adolescent mental health outcomes are cross-sectional (e.g., 12 of 13 studies in Keles et al. [16]), leaving the direction of causality unresolved — it remains unclear whether computer use causes mental health problems, whether adolescents with pre-existing mental health problems gravitate toward greater computer use, or whether a bidirectional relationship exists.
2. "Computer usage" and "screen time" are treated as undifferentiated, monolithic exposures across nearly all retrieved studies, with no systematic comparison of how distinct types of computer activity — such as passive content consumption versus active content creation, social interaction versus solitary gaming, or educational versus recreational use — differentially affect specific mental health outcomes in teenagers.
3. There is a near-total absence of research using objective, device-logged computer usage data (e.g., operating system screen time trackers, application programming interface logs) in adolescent mental health studies; virtually all evidence relies on self-reported usage estimates, which are known to be unreliable and subject to recall and social desirability biases.
4. Very few studies investigate the mediating mechanisms through which computer use might influence mental health — potential pathways such as sleep displacement [21], social comparison, cyberbullying exposure, displacement of physical activity [12, 13], attentional fragmentation, or dopamine-related reward conditioning are discussed in isolation but have not been tested within integrated, multivariate mediation models in adolescent populations.
5. Individual-difference moderators are severely underexplored: there is minimal evidence on how pre-existing mental health vulnerabilities, personality traits (e.g., neuroticism, impulsivity), socioeconomic status, family environment, parental mediation styles, or genetic predispositions interact with computer usage patterns to produce divergent mental health trajectories among teenagers.
6. The literature is heavily skewed toward older adolescents (15–19) and young adults (e.g., college students in [8, 11, 18]), with a striking paucity of research on early adolescents aged 10–14, a developmental window during which computer and internet adoption is accelerating rapidly and during which neurocognitive development may confer distinct vulnerabilities.
7. Longitudinal studies tracking within-individual changes in computer usage patterns and mental health symptoms over extended periods (multiple years spanning key developmental transitions) are essentially absent from the retrieved literature, preventing assessment of cumulative exposure effects or sensitive-period hypotheses.
8. Research on potential beneficial effects of computer use on adolescent mental health — including online social support, identity exploration, access to mental health information and digital interventions [9, 24], creative expression, and civic engagement — is sparse relative to the volume of research focused on harms, creating a one-sided evidence base that cannot support balanced policy recommendations.
9. The geographic and cultural representation of the evidence base is narrow, with the majority of studies drawn from Western, high-income countries (US, UK, Italy, Australia); there is a lack of research from low- and middle-income countries and from non-Western cultural contexts where patterns of computer use, family structures, and mental health stigma may differ substantially.
10. No retrieved study integrates neurobiological measures (e.g., cortisol, heart rate variability, neuroimaging, electroencephalography) with behavioural computer usage data and mental health outcomes in adolescents, leaving the biological embedding of digital technology exposure during this sensitive developmental period entirely uncharacterized.
11. The COVID-19 pandemic studies [5, 8, 19, 25, 28, 29] confound pandemic-related stressors (social isolation, health anxiety, economic disruption) with increased computer and screen use, making it impossible to isolate the independent contribution of computer usage to observed mental health declines — post-pandemic research that disentangles these effects is lacking.
12. There is no research examining the role of computer use context — such as co-use with parents versus solitary use, use in bedroom versus shared household spaces, or use during school hours versus leisure time — as a moderator of the relationship between computer usage and adolescent mental health outcomes.
