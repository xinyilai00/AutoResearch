CANDIDATE RESEARCH QUESTIONS:
1. What are the directionality and temporal dynamics of the relationship between smartphone use intensity and adolescent depressive and anxiety symptoms, as revealed by cross-lagged panel modeling applied to publicly available longitudinal cohort data (e.g., the ABCD Study, UK Millennium Cohort Study)? | Gap addressed: Gap 1 — the overwhelming reliance on cross-sectional designs that cannot determine causal direction | Feasibility: The ABCD Study and UK Millennium Cohort Study provide publicly accessible multi-wave longitudinal data on adolescent screen use and validated mental health measures, enabling cross-lagged panel models and latent growth curve analyses without new data collection.

2. Which specific mediating pathways — including sleep displacement, physical activity reduction, social comparison, and FoMO — most strongly explain the association between adolescent phone use and mental health outcomes, as determined through meta-analytic structural equation modeling of published correlation matrices? | Gap addressed: Gap 5 — the absence of systematically tested integrated causal models of mediating mechanisms | Feasibility: Meta-analytic SEM (MASEM) can be conducted using pooled correlation matrices extracted from the dozens of published studies identified in existing systematic reviews, requiring no new data collection and relying entirely on published effect sizes and open-source statistical software (e.g., metaSEM in R).

3. How do distinct categories of phone activity — passive social media scrolling, active messaging, gaming, video consumption, and educational use — differentially associate with adolescent mental health outcomes, as identified through computational clustering of app-category usage data from public device-logging datasets? | Gap addressed: Gap 3 — the treatment of phone use as a monolithic construct without activity-level differentiation | Feasibility: Publicly available datasets such as the ABCD Study include granular screen activity categories; additionally, anonymized device-logged usage datasets have been released on repositories like OSF and Kaggle, enabling clustering and differential association analyses using computational classification methods.

4. What is the magnitude and pattern of discrepancy between self-reported and objectively logged smartphone screen time among adolescents, and does this measurement error systematically bias the estimated association between phone use and mental health symptoms? | Gap addressed: Gap 4 — the near-exclusive reliance on unreliable self-reported screen time and the scarcity of validation against objective device logs | Feasibility: Several public datasets (e.g., ABCD Study, specific OSF-registered studies) contain both self-reported and device-logged screen time measures for adolescent participants, enabling direct comparison, Bland-Altman analysis, and measurement error correction modeling using standard statistical methods.

5. Does the latent structure of problematic smartphone use in adolescents, as assessed through network psychometric analysis and bifactor modeling of publicly available scale response data, support a distinct behavioral addiction construct or reflect a manifestation of underlying depression, anxiety, and impulsivity? | Gap addressed: Gap 10 — the unresolved conceptual debate over whether smartphone addiction is a genuine behavioral disorder or a symptom of other conditions | Feasibility: Item-level response data from validated scales (SAS-SV, SPAI) administered in large public cohort studies can be analyzed using network psychometrics and bifactor models to test whether problematic use forms a coherent, distinct latent factor or is better explained as a secondary manifestation of established psychopathology dimensions.

6. Which adolescent subgroups — defined by baseline mental health status, socioeconomic status, gender, and family environment — are most vulnerable to adverse mental health effects from high phone use, as identified through moderation and subgroup analyses in large-scale public longitudinal datasets? | Gap addressed: Gap 6 — the lack of research on individual vulnerability and resilience factors that moderate the phone use–mental health relationship | Feasibility: The ABCD Study and UK Millennium Cohort Study include rich demographic, socioeconomic, family environment, and baseline mental health variables alongside screen use data, enabling pre-registered moderation analyses, interaction models, and machine-learning-based subgroup identification without new data collection.

7. To what extent do smartphone addiction scales developed in East Asian university populations demonstrate measurement invariance when applied to younger adolescents and Western populations, as tested through multi-group confirmatory factor analysis on publicly available cross-cultural validation datasets? | Gap addressed: Gap 2 — the absence of culturally adapted and psychometrically validated measurement instruments for younger adolescents and non-East Asian populations | Feasibility: Published validation studies of SAS-SV and SPAI across multiple countries have released item-level data or sufficient summary statistics on public repositories; multi-group CFA and differential item functioning analyses can be conducted on these data to systematically evaluate cross-cultural and cross-age measurement equivalence.

8. What is the comparative efficacy of adolescent-targeted mental health apps versus general-population apps, as estimated through a systematic computational analysis of app features, user engagement metrics from public app store data, and meta-analysis of available trial outcomes? | Gap addressed: Gap 9 — the thin evidence base for mental health apps specifically designed for adolescent populations | Feasibility: Public app store metadata (Google Play, Apple App Store), published RCT results, and open trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, OSF) provide sufficient data for a systematic feature extraction, engagement metric comparison, and meta-analysis of existing efficacy trials, all achievable through computational text mining and standard meta-analytic methods.

9. What long-term developmental trajectories of problematic phone use emerge from early adolescence through early adulthood, and what factors predict persistence versus remission, as modeled through latent class growth analysis on publicly available multi-wave panel data? | Gap addressed: Gap 7 — the near-total absence of long-term prospective cohort studies tracking adolescents from early phone acquisition into adulthood | Feasibility: Existing long-running panel studies (e.g., Add Health, UK Millennium Cohort Study) now span adolescence into early adulthood and include screen use and mental health measures at multiple waves, enabling latent class growth analysis and trajectory modeling on publicly accessible data.

10. Under what conditions do phone-based social connections produce measurable mental health benefits for marginalized or socially isolated adolescents, as identified through secondary analysis of publicly available survey data containing measures of peer support, online community belonging, and psychological well-being? | Gap addressed: Gap 8 — the systematic underexploration of potentially beneficial aspects of phone use for adolescent mental health | Feasibility: Large public surveys such as the CDC Youth Risk Behavior Survey, Pew Research Center teen surveys, and Common Sense Media datasets include items on online social support, community belonging, identity exploration, and well-being indicators, enabling targeted secondary analyses focused on beneficial use patterns rather than exclusively on harm.
