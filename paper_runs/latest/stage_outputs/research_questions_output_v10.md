CANDIDATE RESEARCH QUESTIONS:
1. To what extent can computational feature extraction of harmonic vocabulary (modulation frequency, chromaticism density, remote key relationships, dissonance treatment) from MusicXML-encoded scores statistically differentiate early Beethoven piano sonatas (Opp. 2–28) from late sonatas (Opp. 101–111), and which harmonic features contribute most to period classification? | Gap addressed: Gap 4 (computational/corpus-based methods entirely absent) and Gap 1 (no systematic multi-dimensional comparison) | Feasibility: Complete MusicXML and MIDI encodings of all 32 Beethoven piano sonatas are freely available from MuseScore.com, IMSLP, and the When in Rome corpus; Python libraries music21 and mir_eval enable automated harmonic analysis, and standard classifiers (SVM, random forest) can be trained on extracted features with clear period labels.

2. How do measurable notational parameters—including articulation marking density, dynamic indication frequency, tempo qualification specificity, and explicit fingering annotations—differ between early and late Beethoven piano sonatas when quantified through automated score parsing, and do these differences suggest a systematic shift in Beethoven's prescriptive intent toward performers? | Gap addressed: Gap 3 (notation-based analysis not extended to piano sonatas) and Gap 1 (no unified comparative framework) | Feasibility: High-resolution digitized first editions and autograph manuscript facsimiles are publicly available through IMSLP and the Beethoven-Haus Bonn digital archive; symbolic encodings (MusicXML) allow programmatic counting and categorization of notational elements across all sonatas without manual transcription.

3. Can a computational stylometric pipeline applied to MIDI and MusicXML representations of Beethoven's piano sonatas, string quartets, and symphonies reveal whether the early-to-late stylistic divergence follows parallel trajectories across genres, or whether the piano sonatas exhibit earlier, more pronounced, or qualitatively different transformation? | Gap addressed: Gap 8 (cross-genre comparison absent) and Gap 4 (computational methods absent) | Feasibility: The When in Rome corpus, MuseScore, and the Classical Archives MIDI repository provide complete symbolic data for Beethoven's sonatas, quartets, and symphonies; unified feature extraction (harmonic rhythm, textural density, motivic economy) enables direct cross-genre statistical comparison using publicly available computational tools.

4. What quantitative relationship exists between the documented timeline of Beethoven's hearing deterioration (as established through letters, conversation books, and medical records) and the rate of change in measurable compositional features—such as registral range, textural density, and contrapuntal complexity—across the chronological sequence of piano sonatas? | Gap addressed: Gap 7 (biographical trajectory and measurable stylistic divergence unaddressed) | Feasibility: Beethoven's correspondence and conversation books are digitized and publicly accessible via the Beethoven-Haus Bonn digital collection; the chronological sequence of sonatas with precise dating is well established; computational feature extraction from symbolic scores enables time-series correlation analysis between biographical milestones and stylistic metrics.

5. How do text-mined critical writings about Beethoven's piano sonatas from nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century music journals, reviews, and theoretical treatises construct, reinforce, or contest the categorical distinction between "early" and "late" sonatas, and when did this binary periodization first emerge in critical discourse? | Gap addressed: Gap 5 (reception history of the early-late binary uninvestigated) | Feasibility: Large corpora of digitized public-domain music criticism are available through HathiTrust, JSTOR Early Journal Content, Internet Archive, and the Répertoire International de la Presse Musicale (RIPM); standard NLP methods (topic modeling, sentiment analysis, keyword frequency tracking) can trace the emergence and evolution of periodization language across decades of published criticism.

6. To what extent can algorithmic approximation of Schenkerian structural analysis—using computational voice-leading reduction and hierarchical pitch-space modeling—reveal systematic differences in structural coherence and voice-leading complexity between early and late Beethoven piano sonatas? | Gap addressed: Gap 6 (Schenkerian frameworks not systematically applied to comparative early-late study) and Gap 4 (computational methods absent) | Feasibility: Computational implementations of Schenkerian reduction (e.g., the algorithms described by Marsden, 2010, and tools within music21) can process MusicXML encodings of all 32 sonatas; quantitative metrics such as structural depth, prolongation span, and voice-leading strand count can be extracted and compared statistically between early and late groups.

7. Can computational modeling of emotional-acoustic features (key mode, tempo, note density, spectral energy, dynamic range) extracted from audio recordings and symbolic scores of Beethoven's piano sonatas produce a quantifiable emotional profile that distinguishes early from late works, and how do these profiles align with established musicological characterizations of each period? | Gap addressed: Gap 2 (cognitive/perceptual differentiation unexamined) and Gap 4 (computational methods absent) | Feasibility: Public-domain audio recordings of complete Beethoven sonata cycles are available through Musopen and IMSLP; the Essentia and librosa audio analysis libraries enable automated extraction of emotional-acoustic features; results can be compared against established musicological descriptions from public-domain reference works to assess alignment without requiring human subjects.

8. How does the thematic and motivic economy of late Beethoven piano sonatas—as measured by computational motif detection, intervallic profile analysis, and thematic transformation tracking—compare to that of early sonatas, and does the late period exhibit greater motivic concentration and developmental integration as traditionally claimed in musicological literature? | Gap addressed: Gap 1 (no systematic comparison of thematic development) and Gap 4 (computational methods absent) | Feasibility: Symbolic encodings (MusicXML/MIDI) of all sonatas enable algorithmic motif extraction using n-gram analysis, interval-class distribution, and sequence detection implemented in music21 and humdrum; quantitative comparison of motivic recurrence rates, transformation distances, and developmental density between early and late groups is computationally straightforward with public data.
