SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The literature on AI in the stock market coalesces around several interconnected themes. The most prominent strand applies machine learning and deep learning methods to stock price prediction and asset pricing. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) demonstrated that machine learning forecasts—particularly tree-based models and neural networks—can substantially outperform traditional regression-based strategies in measuring asset risk premiums, effectively doubling investor performance in some cases. Complementing this, multiple studies have applied deep learning architectures to stock market prediction, including LSTM-CNN feature fusion models (Kim & Kim, 2019), deep generative models that jointly exploit textual and price signals (Xu & Cohen, 2018), and comparative analyses of continuous versus binary data representations for trend prediction (Nabipour et al., 2020). Shah et al. (2019) provided a taxonomy of prediction techniques, acknowledging the inherent difficulty of the task given the stochastic, nonlinear, and high-dimensional nature of stock markets. A related strand of work explores sentiment analysis and alternative data sources, with Sohangir et al. (2018) applying deep learning to large-scale financial text data and Xu and Cohen (2018) integrating Twitter data with historical prices through recurrent continuous latent variables.

A second major theme concerns the theoretical tension between traditional finance paradigms and AI-driven prediction. The efficient markets hypothesis (Fama & French, 2004; Shiller, 2003) posits that stock prices already reflect all available information, making systematic prediction inherently difficult. Shiller (2003) documented the gradual erosion of faith in strong-form efficiency and the rise of behavioral finance, while Rabin and Thaler (2001) highlighted anomalies in risk aversion that standard expected-utility frameworks cannot explain. These theoretical foundations create a backdrop against which AI prediction claims must be evaluated: if markets are largely efficient, the economic gains reported by Gu et al. (2020) and others may reflect compensation for risk rather than genuine predictive alpha, or they may capture transient inefficiencies that erode as more participants adopt similar methods.

A third theme encompasses the broader methodological landscape of AI and machine learning that remains largely untapped by the stock market prediction literature. Federated learning (Kairouz et al., 2020), domain adaptation theory (Ben-David et al., 2009), automated machine learning (Hutter et al., 2019), deep reinforcement learning (François-Lavet et al., 2018), and modern ensemble techniques (Mienye & Sun, 2022) have all matured significantly and have been applied in adjacent domains such as healthcare, energy systems, and supply chain management. Yet their application to stock market AI remains sparse or nonexistent. Additionally, the intersection of blockchain and AI (Salah et al., 2019) and the role of AI in digital financial inclusion (Mhlanga, 2020) suggest infrastructure-level innovations that could reshape how stock market prediction systems are built, audited, and deployed, but these connections have not been systematically explored in the financial prediction literature.

GAPS:
1. Federated learning has not been applied to stock market prediction, despite its clear relevance: multiple financial institutions could collaboratively train predictive models without sharing proprietary trading data or violating data privacy regulations, yet no study in the retrieved literature explores this approach in a financial markets context.
2. Domain adaptation and transfer learning across different stock markets, asset classes, or time regimes remain virtually unstudied; the existing prediction papers (e.g., Nabipour et al., 2020) train and evaluate on single markets (such as the Tehran Stock Exchange) with no investigation of whether learned representations generalize to other geographic markets, sectors, or market conditions (bull vs. bear regimes).
3. Deep reinforcement learning, despite being explicitly identified as applicable to finance (François-Lavet et al., 2018), is absent from the stock market prediction literature retrieved here; no paper applies deep RL agents to end-to-end trading strategy optimization that integrates prediction, position sizing, and execution in a unified framework.
4. Automated machine learning (AutoML) has not been applied to stock market prediction tasks, even though the existing studies all rely on manual architecture selection and hyperparameter tuning, leaving open the question of whether AutoML systems could discover superior model configurations for financial time series.
5. There is an unresolved theoretical and empirical contradiction between the efficient markets hypothesis (Fama & French, 2004; Shiller, 2003) and the claims of AI-based predictability (Gu et al., 2020; Nabipour et al., 2020); no paper systematically investigates whether reported AI prediction gains represent genuine alpha, compensation for latent risk factors, or artifacts of backtesting overfitting, nor whether such gains decay as AI methods become widely adopted.
6. Explainability and interpretability of AI models for stock market prediction are entirely absent from the literature; deep learning models (LSTM, CNN, deep generative models) are deployed as black boxes with no attention to feature attribution, model interpretability, or regulatory compliance requirements that are critical in financial decision-making contexts.
7. The geographic and market-cap diversity of studied markets is severely limited, with a disproportionate focus on the Tehran Stock Exchange and U.S. equities, leaving emerging markets, frontier markets, and cross-market comparative analyses almost completely unexplored.
8. Real-time and online learning paradigms—where models continuously update as new data arrives—have not been investigated for stock market prediction, despite the non-stationary nature of financial time series making static trained models inherently prone to concept drift and performance degradation over time.
9. Multimodal data fusion in stock prediction remains narrow, limited to text-plus-price (Xu & Cohen, 2018) or time-series-plus-chart-images (Kim & Kim, 2019), with no studies integrating a broader spectrum of modalities such as earnings call audio, satellite imagery, options chain microstructure data, central bank communications, or macroeconomic indicator streams within a unified deep learning architecture.
10. Risk-adjusted performance evaluation and realistic trading cost modeling are underexplored; most prediction papers report accuracy metrics (MSE, directional accuracy) without translating predictions into portfolio-level returns adjusted for transaction costs, slippage, market impact, and drawdown risk, making it unclear whether reported predictive gains are economically meaningful in practice.
11. Blockchain-AI integration for stock market prediction systems—encompassing model auditability, decentralized prediction markets, tamper-proof backtesting logs, and transparent algorithmic trading records—has not been explored despite the clear relevance of both technologies to financial market infrastructure (Salah et al., 2019).
12. The interaction between AI-driven stock prediction and market microstructure effects (liquidity provision, order book dynamics, flash crash propagation) is unstudied; no paper examines how widespread deployment of similar AI prediction models might create herding behavior, correlated trading, or systemic risk amplification.
