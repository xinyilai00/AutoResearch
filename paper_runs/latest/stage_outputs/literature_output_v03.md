SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The literature on sleep quality and teenager mental health reveals a well-established but methodologically heterogeneous body of evidence linking sleep disruption to adverse psychological outcomes in young people. Foundational work by Owens et al. (2014) and Chaput et al. (2016) documents that insufficient and poor-quality sleep is highly prevalent among adolescents and is associated with a range of negative health indicators including depression, anxiety, impaired cognitive function, and reduced academic performance. Alvaro et al. (2013) provide critical evidence that the relationship between sleep disturbances and both anxiety and depression is bidirectional, meaning that poor sleep can precipitate mental health problems and, conversely, psychological distress can degrade sleep quality. Medić et al. (2017) further elaborate on the short- and long-term physiological consequences of sleep disruption, noting its effects on neurocognitive function, emotional regulation, and systemic health. Ohayon et al. (2004) contribute normative data on sleep parameters across the lifespan, establishing that adolescents experience distinct shifts in sleep architecture, including changes in REM sleep and sleep latency, which may interact with the neurodevelopmental changes characteristic of this period (Sharma et al., 2013).

A second major theme concerns the role of technology and digital media as a mediating factor in the sleep–mental health nexus among adolescents. Demirci et al. (2015) and Thomée et al. (2011) demonstrate that greater smartphone use severity is associated with poorer sleep quality, elevated stress, and higher levels of depression and anxiety in young adults. Keles et al. (2019) systematically review the influence of social media on adolescent depression and anxiety, while Stiglic and Viner (2019) synthesize evidence on broader screen time effects, finding consistent associations with sleep displacement and psychological distress. The COVID-19 pandemic has intensified these concerns: Racine et al. (2021) and Ravens-Sieberer et al. (2021) document substantial increases in clinically elevated depression and anxiety symptoms among children and adolescents globally, while Stanton et al. (2020) show that pandemic-related changes in sleep, physical activity, and other health behaviors are significantly associated with psychological distress.

A third theme encompasses the broader lifestyle and biological context. Lubans et al. (2016) and Tremblay et al. (2011) examine physical activity and sedentary behavior as modifiable factors influencing youth mental health, and Firth et al. (2020) provide a meta-review positioning sleep alongside exercise, diet, and smoking within a "lifestyle psychiatry" framework. Besedovsky et al. (2019) describe the bidirectional crosstalk between sleep and immune function, suggesting a potential biological pathway through which sleep disruption may affect mental health. Methodological contributions from Ancoli-Israel et al. (2003) on actigraphy and from Fried and Nesse (2015) on the limitations of depression sum-scores highlight ongoing challenges in how sleep and mental health outcomes are measured and conceptualized in this population.

GAPS:
1. The vast majority of large-scale studies in adolescent populations use sleep duration as the primary or sole sleep metric, while sleep quality dimensions—such as sleep fragmentation, sleep efficiency, sleep architecture (proportions of REM, deep, and light sleep), and subjective sleep quality—are rarely examined as independent predictors of mental health outcomes. This conflation of duration and quality obscures which specific aspects of sleep are most consequential for adolescent psychological well-being.
2. There is a striking absence of randomized controlled trials or quasi-experimental intervention studies that test whether improving sleep quality (through behavioral sleep hygiene programs, school-based schedule changes, cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia, or digital sleep interventions) causally reduces depression or anxiety symptoms in adolescent populations. The existing evidence base is overwhelmingly cross-sectional or correlational, limiting causal inference.
3. The specific neurobiological mechanisms linking sleep quality to adolescent mental health outcomes remain poorly characterized. While Besedovsky et al. (2019) describe sleep-immune crosstalk and Sharma et al. (2013) describe adolescent brain maturation, no studies integrate these domains to examine whether sleep disruption during adolescence affects mental health through specific pathways such as HPA axis dysregulation, neuroinflammation, prefrontal cortex development, or amygdala reactivity in this age group.
4. The role of circadian rhythm disruption—particularly social jet lag, delayed sleep-wake phase, and circadian misalignment—in adolescent mental health is substantially underexplored relative to simple sleep duration or subjective sleep quality. Given that adolescents undergo a well-documented biological shift toward eveningness, the mental health consequences of the mismatch between biological and social schedules represent a critical understudied area.
5. Intersectional demographic moderators of the sleep–mental health relationship in adolescents are largely unexamined. While Piccinelli and Wilkinson (2000) document gender differences in depression and Satcher (2001) highlights the role of culture, race, and ethnicity in mental health, these factors have not been systematically integrated into research on sleep and adolescent mental health. The differential impact of sleep quality across socioeconomic strata, racial/ethnic groups, gender identities, and neurodivergent adolescents remains a significant gap.
6. Fried and Nesse (2015) demonstrate that depression sum-scores mask symptom-specific effects, yet no studies have examined whether sleep quality differentially predicts specific depression or anxiety symptoms (e.g., anhedonia vs. suicidal ideation, or generalized worry vs. panic) in adolescent populations. This symptom-level analysis could reveal more precise targets for intervention.
7. While individual lifestyle factors—sleep, physical activity, sedentary behavior, screen time, and diet—are each studied in isolation, their interactive and combined effects on adolescent mental health are poorly understood. The WHO guidelines (Bull et al., 2020) and the lifestyle psychiatry framework (Firth et al., 2020) call for integrated approaches, but empirical studies examining how sleep quality interacts with physical activity levels, screen time, and dietary patterns to jointly influence adolescent mental health are virtually nonexistent.
8. Large-scale studies using objective sleep measurement (actigraphy or wearable devices) in naturalistic, real-world adolescent settings are scarce. Ancoli-Israel et al. (2003) validate actigraphy primarily in adult and clinical populations, and most adolescent sleep research relies on self-report measures, which are subject to recall bias and may poorly capture sleep fragmentation and micro-arousals that are potentially relevant to mental health outcomes.
9. The post-pandemic recovery trajectory of adolescent sleep and mental health is entirely unstudied in the retrieved literature. While Racine et al. (2021) and Ravens-Sieberer et al. (2021) document the acute mental health impact of COVID-19, and Stanton et al. (2020) note concurrent sleep changes, no longitudinal studies track whether adolescents whose sleep quality improves during post-pandemic recovery also experience corresponding improvements in mental health, which would strengthen causal inference.
10. The potential mediating or moderating role of the school environment—including school start times, homework load, extracurricular scheduling, and sleep education curricula—in the relationship between sleep quality and adolescent mental health is underexplored. While Owens et al. (2014) mention school start times as a contributor to adolescent sleep loss, systematic research on how institutional factors interact with individual sleep quality to shape mental health outcomes is lacking.
