SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature on caffeine and cognitive function reveals a well-established body of evidence confirming that caffeine, primarily through adenosine receptor antagonism (A1 and A2A), exerts measurable effects on alertness, vigilance, and sustained attention. Foundational work by Nehlig (2010) and Brice & Smith (2001) demonstrates that caffeine reliably improves performance on vigilance tasks and sustained-response paradigms, particularly under conditions of low arousal such as sleep deprivation or prolonged wakefulness. The mechanistic basis for these effects is articulated in Reichert et al. (2022), who trace the adenosinergic system's role in sleep-wake regulation and explain how caffeine's competitive binding at adenosine receptors promotes wakefulness and alertness. The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand (Guest et al., 2021) and Spriet (2014) further corroborate that even low doses of caffeine (~3 mg/kg or ~200 mg) produce meaningful cognitive and performance benefits, with the literature converging on the finding that caffeine's most robust cognitive effects are on attention and alertness rather than on learning or higher-order memory processes.

A second major theme concerns the bidirectional relationship between caffeine consumption, sleep disruption, and downstream cognitive functioning. Multiple studies document that caffeine impairs sleep quality and architecture (O'Callaghan et al., 2018; Bonnet & Arand, 1992), while chronic sleep restriction produces cumulative neurobehavioral deficits that include degraded attention (Van Dongen et al., 2003). This creates a paradoxical cycle in which caffeine is used to counteract attentional deficits that caffeine itself may exacerbate through sleep interference. The literature also extends into caffeine's neuroprotective potential, with several reviews linking chronic caffeine consumption to reduced risk or delayed onset of Alzheimer's disease (Arendash & Cao, 2010; Ikram et al., 2020) and Parkinson's disease (Ren & Chen, 2020), suggesting that caffeine's attentional benefits may be part of a broader neuropharmacological profile with long-term implications.

A third cluster of work situates caffeine within a broader landscape of environmental and physiological modulators of cognition, including light exposure (Chellappa et al., 2011), hydration status (Masento et al., 2014), physical activity (Haverkamp et al., 2020; Kramer et al., 2004), nutritional factors (Gibson & Green, 2002), and serotonin function (Schmitt et al., 2006). These studies collectively establish that attention is multiply determined and that caffeine operates alongside — and potentially in interaction with — a range of other modifiable factors. Research on alternative caffeine delivery forms (Wickham & Spriet, 2018) and energy drinks (McLellan & Lieberman, 2012) further explores how route of administration and co-ingredients may modulate caffeine's attentional effects, though the evidence for contributions beyond caffeine itself remains limited.

GAPS:
1. Nearly all studies measuring caffeine's effect on attention rely on short-duration laboratory tasks (vigilance, reaction time, psychomotor vigilance test) lasting minutes to a few hours; there is a striking absence of research examining caffeine's effects on sustained attention across ecologically valid timeframes such as full workdays, academic sessions, or multi-day periods, leaving it unclear whether laboratory gains translate to real-world attentional endurance.
2. The literature is heavily skewed toward healthy young adult males, with very limited investigation of caffeine's attentional effects in adolescents (despite rising caffeine consumption in this population), older adults (where attention decline is clinically meaningful), and across sex and gender differences — the ABCD study (Barch et al., 2017) provides infrastructure for adolescent cognitive research but has not been leveraged for caffeine-specific attention analyses.
3. Individual differences in caffeine metabolism driven by CYP1A2 genotype, habitual consumption patterns, and tolerance development are acknowledged in pharmacokinetic reviews (DePaula & Farah, 2019; Temple et al., 2017) but are almost never incorporated as moderating variables in attention outcome studies, leaving unresolved the question of for whom caffeine is most and least effective as an attentional enhancer.
4. The interaction between caffeine and sleep deprivation on attention has been studied in broad strokes, but the dose-response dynamics remain poorly characterized — specifically, the threshold at which caffeine can no longer compensate for accumulated sleep debt, and whether repeated caffeine dosing across a period of sleep restriction produces diminishing attentional returns, have not been systematically mapped.
5. There is virtually no research using modern neuroimaging methods (fMRI, EEG source localization, MEG) to identify the specific neural circuits and network-level dynamics through which caffeine modulates attention; the existing cognitive literature operates almost entirely at the behavioral level without mechanistic neural evidence.
6. Studies have not adequately disentangled caffeine's effects on different subtypes of attention (selective attention, divided attention, executive attention, attentional switching) — most research collapses these into a single "vigilance" or "sustained attention" construct, obscuring potentially differential effects across attentional subsystems.
7. The comparative effectiveness of different caffeine delivery forms (coffee, capsules, chewing gum, energy drinks, gels) on attention specifically has not been rigorously tested in within-subjects designs that control for dose, rate of absorption, and expectancy effects, despite the proliferation of novel caffeine products in the consumer market.
8. No studies have examined the interaction between caffeine and other modifiable environmental factors (light exposure, hydration, physical activity, ambient temperature) on attention in factorial designs, even though the literature independently establishes each of these as cognitive modulators — the potential for synergistic or antagonistic interactions remains entirely unexplored.
9. The long-term trajectory of caffeine's attentional effects under chronic daily use — including whether tolerance eliminates attentional benefits, whether withdrawal periods restore sensitivity, and whether chronic use produces net attentional gains or merely restores baseline — remains a subject of theoretical debate without adequate longitudinal or repeated-measures evidence to resolve it.
10. Research has not addressed caffeine's effects on attention in the context of modern digital environments characterized by frequent interruptions, multitasking demands, and notification-driven task switching, creating a gap between the controlled vigilance paradigms used in existing studies and the attentional challenges people actually use caffeine to manage.
