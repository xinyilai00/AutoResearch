CANDIDATE RESEARCH QUESTIONS:
1. Does caffeine produce differential effects across distinct attentional subtypes (selective attention, divided attention, executive attention, and attentional switching) when task-specific effect sizes are disaggregated through meta-analysis? | Gap addressed: Gap 6 — existing research collapses attention into a single vigilance construct, obscuring differential effects across attentional subsystems. | Feasibility: A systematic meta-analysis can be conducted using published effect sizes from studies employing distinct attention paradigms (Stroop, flanker, task-switching, dual-task, visual search), all of which are reported in publicly available journal articles and preprint repositories.

2. At what cumulative sleep-debt threshold does caffeine fail to restore sustained attention performance, as predicted by integrated pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) modeling of adenosine receptor occupancy and sleep homeostasis? | Gap addressed: Gap 4 — dose-response dynamics of caffeine-sleep deprivation interaction on attention remain poorly characterized, particularly the compensation ceiling. | Feasibility: PK/PD parameters for caffeine (absorption, half-life, receptor affinity) and sleep homeostasis models (Process S) are published in open-access pharmacological and sleep research literature; computational simulation can map the interaction surface across caffeine doses and sleep-debt levels without human subjects.

3. What is the association between habitual caffeine consumption patterns and performance on attention tasks among adolescents aged 9-14, as measured in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study? | Gap addressed: Gap 2 — the literature is heavily skewed toward healthy young adult males, with minimal investigation of caffeine's attentional effects in adolescents despite rising consumption in this population. | Feasibility: The ABCD study is a publicly accessible dataset (via NDA/NIH) containing cognitive task performance data, substance use surveys including caffeine intake, and demographic variables for over 11,000 adolescents, enabling observational analysis without new data collection.

4. Can CYP1A2 genotype-stratified pharmacokinetic simulations predict the magnitude and duration of caffeine's attentional enhancement across population subgroups defined by metabolizer status (slow, intermediate, fast)? | Gap addressed: Gap 3 — individual differences in caffeine metabolism are acknowledged in pharmacokinetic reviews but almost never incorporated as moderating variables in attention outcome studies. | Feasibility: CYP1A2 allele frequency data are available from public genomic databases (1000 Genomes, gnomAD), caffeine pharmacokinetic parameters by genotype are published in open-access pharmacology literature, and Monte Carlo simulations can model expected attentional response distributions across metabolizer classes.

5. Can computational cognitive architecture models (e.g., ACT-R or drift-diffusion models) calibrated on short-duration laboratory data extrapolate caffeine's attentional effects to predict performance degradation curves across ecologically valid timeframes of 4-8 hours? | Gap addressed: Gap 1 — nearly all studies use short-duration tasks lasting minutes to a few hours, leaving it unclear whether laboratory gains translate to real-world attentional endurance across full workdays. | Feasibility: Published drift-diffusion and ACT-R model parameters for vigilance tasks under caffeine are available in open-access cognitive modeling literature; simulations can extend predictions to longer timeframes by integrating pharmacokinetic decay curves and fatigue accumulation functions.

6. Does chronic daily caffeine consumption produce tolerance to its attentional benefits, as quantified by comparing acute versus habitual user effect sizes across published controlled trials? | Gap addressed: Gap 9 — the long-term trajectory of caffeine's attentional effects under chronic use remains theoretically debated without adequate longitudinal evidence to resolve whether tolerance eliminates benefits. | Feasibility: A meta-analytic comparison of effect sizes from studies testing caffeine-naive versus caffeine-habituated participants can be conducted using publicly available published results, with habitual intake levels and washout periods as moderator variables.

7. Do different caffeine delivery forms (coffee, capsules, gum, energy drinks) produce measurably different attentional outcomes when dose is held constant, as revealed through meta-regression of published within- and between-subjects studies? | Gap addressed: Gap 7 — comparative effectiveness of delivery forms on attention has not been rigorously tested in designs controlling for dose and absorption rate. | Feasibility: Published studies using various caffeine delivery methods report sufficient methodological detail (dose, timing, attention outcomes) to enable meta-regression analysis using publicly available journal articles, with delivery form, absorption rate estimates, and dose as predictors.

8. What are the predicted interactive effects of caffeine with co-occurring modifiable factors (blue-light exposure, hydration status, acute physical activity) on attention performance, as modeled through computational integration of independently established dose-response functions? | Gap addressed: Gap 8 — no studies have examined caffeine's interaction with other environmental cognitive modulators in factorial designs, leaving synergistic or antagonistic interactions unexplored. | Feasibility: Independent dose-response curves for each factor's effect on attention are published in open-access literature; computational integration using response-surface methodology and additive/interactive models can generate testable predictions about combined effects without requiring new experiments.

9. Can analysis of publicly available resting-state and task-based fMRI datasets that include caffeine administration reveal network-level changes in frontoparietal attention circuitry and default-mode network suppression? | Gap addressed: Gap 5 — virtually no research uses modern neuroimaging to identify neural circuits through which caffeine modulates attention; existing work operates almost entirely at the behavioral level. | Feasibility: Public neuroimaging repositories (OpenNeuro, Human Connectome Project, OpenfMRI) contain datasets with pharmacological challenge designs including caffeine; standardized preprocessing pipelines (fMRIPrep) and network analysis tools enable circuit-level analysis without new scanning.

10. Can computational agent-based modeling of interruption-rich digital work environments simulate how caffeine-modulated attention parameters (switching cost, re-engagement latency, error rate) affect task completion efficiency under varying notification frequencies? | Gap addressed: Gap 10 — no research has addressed caffeine's effects on attention in modern digital environments characterized by frequent interruptions and notification-driven task switching. | Feasibility: Parameters for interruption cost, task-switching overhead, and caffeine's effects on switching speed are available from published laboratory studies; agent-based simulations can model productivity outcomes across interruption-frequency conditions using synthetic task streams without requiring human participants or proprietary workplace data.
