SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature reveals a well-established body of research on non-pharmacological interventions for dementia, with music-based therapies occupying an increasingly prominent position. Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses (van der Steen et al., 2018; Abraha et al., 2017; de Oliveira et al., 2015) have catalogued the growing adoption of music interventions as first-line approaches for managing behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD), including agitation, anxiety, depression, and apathy. These reviews consistently note that while music interventions show promise, the evidence base remains heterogeneous, with mixed results across studies attributable to methodological variability, small sample sizes, and inconsistent outcome measures. The pharmacological treatment landscape—dominated by cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine—provides important context, as these drugs offer only symptomatic relief without disease modification (Yiannopoulou & Papageorgiou, 2020; Doody et al., 2001), reinforcing the urgency of developing effective non-pharmacological alternatives.

A second major theme concerns the diversity of music intervention modalities studied. Researchers have examined active approaches such as group singing (Kim Cho, 2018), percussion-based interventions with familiar music (Sung et al., 2011), piano practice and music learning (Seinfeld et al., 2013), and music-with-movement protocols (Cheung et al., 2016), alongside receptive approaches including personalized playlist listening (Garrido et al., 2017), therapeutic music listening (Raglio, 2023), and individualized music programs (Sakamoto et al., 2013). Some studies have extended into multimodal sensory stimulation, incorporating tactile low-frequency vibration (Campbell et al., 2022), supportive soundscapes in nursing home environments (Devos et al., 2019), and virtual reality therapy (Appel et al., 2020). Neurobiological foundations for music's therapeutic effects have been articulated through evidence of widespread bilateral brain activation during music listening (Särkämö et al., 2008) and calls to ground music-based interventions in advances from neuroscience, psychology, and psychiatry (Bowling, 2023).

A third theme centers on the populations and outcomes studied. Most intervention trials have targeted individuals with mild to moderate dementia in institutional care settings, with primary outcomes focused on BPSD reduction, quality of life, and cognitive function (Bleibel et al., 2023; Leggieri et al., 2019; Bahar-Fuchs et al., 2019). A smaller number of studies have examined effects on caregivers (Hsu et al., 2015; Aligizakis et al., 2021) or explored meaningful engagement as a quality-of-life dimension (Kemp et al., 2023). The distinction between therapist-led and non-therapist-led interventions has emerged as an important consideration (Garrido et al., 2017), though consensus on optimal delivery models remains elusive.

GAPS:
1. No study in the retrieved literature specifically isolates classical music as a genre for dementia intervention; all music-based studies use "music" generically, "familiar music," or "personalized playlists" without genre-level analysis, leaving the specific therapeutic properties of classical music (e.g., structural complexity, harmonic patterns, tempo consistency, absence of lyrics) entirely unexamined.
2. There is a complete absence of comparative studies examining classical music against other genres (e.g., jazz, folk, pop, ambient) in dementia populations, making it impossible to determine whether any observed benefits are genre-specific or attributable to music exposure in general.
3. The neurobiological mechanisms by which classical music specifically might affect neural pathways in dementia remain unexplored; while general music-neuroscience foundations exist (Bowling, 2023; Särkämö et al., 2008), no study has used neuroimaging, EEG, or biomarker analysis to examine classical music's effects on brain structure or function in dementia patients.
4. Dose-response relationships for music interventions—including optimal session duration, frequency, total intervention length, and timing relative to symptom presentation—have not been systematically investigated for any music genre, let alone classical music specifically.
5. The effects of music interventions on individuals with severe or late-stage dementia remain significantly underexplored, as acknowledged by Sakamoto et al. (2013), with most trials excluding this population despite their high symptom burden and limited pharmacological options.
6. Long-term follow-up data are largely absent; nearly all studies report outcomes immediately post-intervention or within weeks, leaving the sustainability of any therapeutic effects—and the question of whether cumulative exposure to classical music produces lasting cognitive or behavioral changes—unanswered.
7. The interaction between music-based interventions and standard pharmacological treatments (cholinesterase inhibitors, memantine) has not been systematically studied, leaving clinicians without evidence on whether music therapy is complementary, additive, or potentially interactive with drug therapies.
8. Caregiver outcomes—including caregiver burden, stress reduction, and quality of life—have received minimal attention in music intervention studies, despite the well-documented impact of BPSD on caregiver wellbeing and the potential for music to serve as a shared therapeutic experience.
9. Cross-cultural and cross-linguistic applicability of music interventions is virtually unstudied; the literature is predominantly Western-centric, and the universal versus culture-specific therapeutic value of classical music (particularly Western art music) in diverse dementia populations remains an open question.
10. Standardized, replicable protocols for classical music interventions do not exist; the field lacks consensus on repertoire selection criteria, delivery format (live vs. recorded), listener engagement level (passive listening vs. active participation), and environmental conditions, impeding both clinical translation and meta-analytic synthesis.
