SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature forms three loosely connected clusters rather than a cohesive body of work on coffee and teenager mental health. The first cluster addresses caffeine pharmacology and safety broadly: Cappelletti et al. (2014) review caffeine's dual nature as both a cognitive/performance enhancer and a psychoactive substance with anxiogenic and cardiovascular effects, while Temple et al. (2017) provide a comprehensive safety review noting the proliferation of new caffeine-containing products and changing consumption patterns. The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand (Guest et al., 2021) and Grgić et al. (2018) focus narrowly on caffeine as an ergogenic aid for muscle strength and power. O'Callaghan et al. (2018) examine caffeine's effects on sleep quality and daytime functioning in adults, establishing that caffeine disrupts sleep architecture — a finding with clear but unstudied implications for adolescents. Notably, none of these caffeine-focused papers disaggregate findings for adolescent populations or specifically examine coffee as a beverage distinct from isolated caffeine administration.

The second cluster concerns adolescent sleep and mental health, but operates largely independently of caffeine research. Multiple papers establish that sleep disruption is pervasive among adolescents and is bidirectionally linked to anxiety and depression (Alvaro et al., 2013; Owens et al., 2014; Bruce et al., 2017; Chaput et al., 2016). Medić et al. (2017) detail the short- and long-term physiological consequences of sleep disruption across body systems. The adolescent brain maturation literature (Sharma et al., 2013; Barch et al., 2017) highlights that the prefrontal cortex — critical for impulse regulation and emotional processing — is still developing during this period, suggesting heightened vulnerability to psychoactive substances. Khalid et al. (2016) systematically review diet-depression associations in children and adolescents but find the evidence base limited and methodologically heterogeneous. Chronotype research (Kivelä et al., 2018) links evening-type patterns to adverse mental health outcomes, a finding relevant to caffeine's potential to delay sleep phase in adolescents.

The third cluster consists of clinical practice guidelines for hypertension (Williams et al., 2018), cardiovascular disease prevention (Visseren et al., 2021), bipolar disorder (Yatham et al., 2018; Goodwin et al., 2016), anxiety disorders (Katzman et al., 2014), inflammatory bowel disease (Lamb et al., 2019), and cancer prevention nutrition (Kushi et al., 2006). While these guidelines address lifestyle factors including diet, caffeine, and sleep in adult populations, none specifically address adolescent caffeine or coffee consumption as a modifiable factor in mental health trajectories. The remaining papers address medical student stress and sleep (Almojali et al., 2017), physical activity and screen time in Chinese college students (Feng et al., 2014), caffeine in pregnancy (Peacock et al., 2018), elite athlete mental health (Reardon et al., 2019), youth athletic development (Bergeron et al., 2015), ADHD sleep interventions (Hiscock et al., 2015), and epidemiological reporting standards (Vandenbroucke et al., 2007).

GAPS:
1. No retrieved study directly investigates the association between coffee consumption and mental health outcomes (depression, anxiety, mood disorders) specifically in adolescent populations (ages 10-19). The literature treats caffeine and adolescent mental health as separate domains with virtually no intersection studied.
2. The developmental vulnerability of the adolescent brain to caffeine's psychoactive effects — particularly given ongoing prefrontal cortex myelination and dopaminergic system maturation — remains almost entirely unexamined in human epidemiological or clinical studies.
3. The mediating pathway from coffee consumption to adolescent mental health via sleep disruption is strongly implied by separate literatures (caffeine impairs sleep; poor sleep causes adolescent depression/anxiety) but has not been formally tested as a causal chain in any retrieved study.
4. Existing caffeine safety reviews (Temple et al., 2017) and position stands (Guest et al., 2021) do not provide age-stratified analyses or adolescent-specific dosage thresholds for adverse mental health effects, leaving a critical gap in evidence-based consumption guidelines for this age group.
5. No study distinguishes the mental health effects of coffee as a whole beverage (containing polyphenols, chlorogenic acids, and other bioactive compounds) from those of isolated caffeine in adolescent populations, despite adult literature suggesting differential effects.
6. The interaction between coffee consumption and chronotype in adolescents — particularly whether evening-type adolescents use coffee to compensate for sleep phase delay, thereby worsening both sleep and mental health — has not been investigated.
7. Longitudinal data on adolescent coffee consumption patterns and subsequent mental health trajectories into young adulthood are entirely absent from the retrieved literature; all relevant studies are cross-sectional or focus on adult populations.
8. Potential sex differences in the relationship between coffee consumption and adolescent mental health remain unexplored, despite known sex differences in both caffeine metabolism and adolescent depression/anxiety prevalence.
9. The dose-response relationship between coffee intake and specific mental health symptom dimensions (e.g., anxiety sensitivity, anhedonia, irritability) in adolescents has not been characterized, leaving clinicians without evidence-based guidance.
10. No retrieved study examines coffee consumption as a confounding or moderating variable in established adolescent mental health risk factors such as screen time, physical activity, or academic stress, despite coffee being a modifiable behavioral factor that co-occurs with these exposures.
