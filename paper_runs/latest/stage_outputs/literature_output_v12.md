SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature reveals several interconnected research streams relevant to the intersection of music and dementia. A substantial body of work examines music-based interventions for people with dementia, encompassing both pre-recorded music listening protocols and active music therapy approaches. Garrido et al. (2017) synthesized 28 studies using pre-recorded music playlists, highlighting the distinction between therapist-led and non-therapist-led interventions and the need for greater methodological rigor. More recently, Prick et al. (2024) conducted a randomized controlled trial evaluating both individualized music therapy and music listening interventions in nursing home settings, targeting neuropsychiatric symptoms and quality of life. Complementary qualitative work by Coomans (2016) explored musical improvisation with persons with severe dementia, identifying moments of resonance that suggest meaningful engagement even at advanced disease stages. Mitsudome (2013) contributed a reliability-validated assessment tool for music therapy in dementia populations. These intervention studies collectively demonstrate that music can positively influence behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia, though effect sizes vary and the specific active ingredients remain poorly characterized.

A second major theme concerns the neurobiological foundations linking music processing, social cognition, and neurodegeneration. Van't Hooft et al. (2021) performed a meta-analysis demonstrating that frontotemporal dementia, music perception, and social cognition share overlapping neurobiological circuits, particularly in fronto-insular and temporal regions. Harvey (2020) examined the links between oxytocin neurobiology and human musicality, proposing that music engages reward and social bonding systems with measurable neurochemical consequences. Bowling (2023) advanced a biological principles framework for understanding how music affects mental health, integrating neuroscience, psychology, and psychiatry to argue that music-based interventions remain marginalized in medicine despite growing biological evidence. Hennessy et al. (2025) demonstrated that music-evoked nostalgia activates default mode and reward networks across the lifespan, with potential implications for autobiographical memory retrieval in cognitive decline. These works collectively establish that music processing is deeply embedded in neural systems affected by dementia, providing a mechanistic rationale for music-based interventions.

A third theme encompasses broader non-pharmacological and technological approaches to dementia and neurodegenerative disease management. These include cognitive training via computerized and virtual reality tools (Lasaponara et al., 2021), robot-assisted psychomotor therapy (Rouaix et al., 2017), dance-based social engagement programs (Kontos et al., 2020), and supportive soundscape design in care environments (Devos et al., 2019). Parallel work on EEG-based affective analysis (Parameshwara et al., 2024; Gkintoni & Halkiopoulos, 2025) and brain-computer interfaces (Zhang et al., 2024) opens avenues for objective neurophysiological measurement of intervention effects. Notably, Park et al. (2015) directly compared preferred music against classical "relaxation" music for agitation following traumatic brain injury, finding that preferred music was more effective—one of the few studies to differentiate music genres within a clinical neurological population. However, across the entire corpus, classical music as a specific genre or category of intervention is strikingly absent from dedicated investigation.

GAPS:
1. No study in the retrieved corpus specifically investigates classical music as a distinct intervention for dementia. While music-based interventions broadly are well-represented, the specific properties of classical music—its structural complexity, dynamic range, harmonic richness, and lack of lyrical content—have not been isolated as variables of interest in dementia research.
2. There is a complete absence of comparative genre studies within dementia populations that pit classical music against other genres (e.g., jazz, folk, popular music, ambient) to determine whether genre-specific features produce differential cognitive, behavioral, or emotional outcomes.
3. No study has employed neurophysiological measures (EEG, fMRI, or autonomic nervous system monitoring) to capture real-time brain responses to classical music specifically in individuals with dementia, despite the availability of validated EEG-based affective and cognitive frameworks in adjacent neurological populations such as Parkinson's disease.
4. The differential effects of classical music across dementia subtypes (Alzheimer's disease, frontotemporal dementia, Lewy body dementia, vascular dementia) remain entirely unexplored. Given that these subtypes involve distinct patterns of neural atrophy and network disruption—as demonstrated by Ng et al. (2021) for AD versus bvFTD—it is plausible that responses to classical music would vary substantially, yet no subtype-stratified analysis exists.
5. The neurochemical mechanisms through which classical music might influence dementia-related symptoms—particularly involving oxytocin, dopamine, cortisol, and other stress-reward biomarkers—have not been empirically tested, despite Harvey (2020) and Bowling (2023) establishing theoretical frameworks that invite such investigation.
6. The role of prior musical training, lifelong classical music exposure, or cultural familiarity with the Western classical tradition as moderating variables in music-based dementia interventions has not been systematically examined. Individual differences in musical background may fundamentally alter intervention efficacy, yet this dimension is largely ignored in existing studies.
7. There is a contradiction in the broader literature regarding music selection: Park et al. (2015) found that personally preferred music outperformed classical "relaxation" music for agitation in TBI patients, yet classical music continues to be widely assumed to have inherent therapeutic properties in dementia care contexts. This tension has not been resolved through direct experimental comparison in dementia populations.
8. Long-term longitudinal studies examining whether sustained exposure to classical music can slow cognitive decline, preserve autobiographical memory, or delay institutionalization in dementia are entirely absent from the literature. Existing intervention studies are predominantly short-term with limited follow-up periods.
9. The potential of classical music to specifically engage the default mode network and support autobiographical memory retrieval in dementia—suggested by Hennessy et al. (2025) for nostalgia-evoking music generally—has not been tested with classical music as the stimulus, despite its strong associations with personal and emotional memory in the general population.
10. The integration of classical music into multimodal non-pharmacological intervention protocols (e.g., combined with cognitive training, physical exercise, or soundscape design) has not been studied, leaving open the question of whether classical music functions best as a standalone intervention or as a synergistic component of broader therapeutic programs.
