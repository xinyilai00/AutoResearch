RELEVANT METHODOLOGIES:

- **Multimodal fusion architectures for imaging + EHR**: (Huang et al., 2020) conducted a systematic review of deep learning approaches that fuse medical imaging with electronic health records, identifying three primary fusion strategies: early fusion (concatenating raw or low-level features), late fusion (combining model outputs or high-level representations), and joint/hybrid fusion (intermediate feature integration via attention or gating mechanisms). They found that most studies employed late fusion and that fewer than a handful of studies rigorously compared multimodal models against optimized unimodal baselines under controlled conditions.

- **Multitask learning with standardized clinical benchmarks on MIMIC**: (Harutyunyan et al., 2019) proposed a multitask learning framework and released standardized benchmark tasks on MIMIC-III clinical time series data, including in-hospital mortality prediction, decompensation prediction, length-of-stay estimation, and phenotype classification. They established reproducible train/validation/test splits and baseline results using LSTM and standard feature engineering pipelines, providing the foundational experimental protocol that subsequent MIMIC-based mortality prediction studies have adopted. Their mortality prediction task achieved AUROC values in the range of 0.84–0.87 using structured EHR data alone.

- **Combining structured and unstructured EHR data with deep learning**: (Zhang et al., 2020) developed a deep learning approach that jointly models temporal structured EHR variables (lab values, vitals, medications) and unstructured clinical notes for clinical outcome prediction. They used a dual-encoder architecture with an RNN or Transformer branch for structured time series and a separate NLP encoder (e.g., ClinicalBERT or BiLSTM) for clinical notes, followed by a fusion layer. They demonstrated that the combined model outperformed either modality alone on prediction tasks within the EHR, though their evaluation was not conducted on MIMIC-IV specifically and did not include imaging.

- **Multimodal Transformer-based architectures**: (Xu et al., 2023) surveyed Transformer-based multimodal learning methods, covering cross-attention fusion, modality-specific encoders with shared Transformer backbones, and pretraining strategies such as masked modality modeling and contrastive alignment. These architectures are directly applicable to combining imaging (via Vision Transformer or CNN encoders), structured data (via tabular embeddings), and clinical text (via clinical language models), and represent the current state-of-the-art design space for multimodal clinical models.

- **Standardized EHR preprocessing with FIDDLE**: (Tang et al., 2020) developed FIDDLE (Flexible Data-Driven preprocessing pipeline for EHR), a systematic and reproducible framework for transforming raw structured EHR data into ML-ready feature matrices. FIDDLE addresses the substantial preprocessing decisions (temporal aggregation, missing value imputation, feature encoding) that can introduce variability across studies, and is directly relevant to establishing a standardized experimental protocol for structured EHR variables in MIMIC-IV.

- **Clinical NLP and information extraction from clinical notes**: (Savova et al., 2008) reviewed methods for extracting structured information from EHR narrative text, establishing the foundation for NLP pipelines applied to clinical notes. (Sheikhalishahi et al., 2019) systematically reviewed NLP approaches for clinical notes in chronic disease, finding that deep learning methods (particularly BiLSTM-CRF and Transformer-based models) outperformed traditional NLP for entity extraction and relation identification. (Jagannatha & Yu, 2016) demonstrated that structured prediction models using RNNs for sequence labeling in clinical text achieved significant improvements in F1 score over CRF baselines for extracting medical entities from EHR narratives. These methods form the basis for encoding clinical notes as a modality in multimodal mortality prediction.

- **Evaluation under class imbalance**: (McDermott et al., 2024) rigorously analyzed the behavior of AUROC and AUPRC under class imbalance conditions typical of in-hospital mortality prediction (where mortality rates are often 5–15%). They theoretically and empirically demonstrated that the common claim that AUPRC is categorically superior to AUROC under imbalance is overstated, and that both metrics capture different aspects of model performance. They recommended reporting both metrics alongside calibration measures, which is critical guidance for any standardized evaluation protocol for mortality prediction.

- **Multimodal deep learning for disease prediction**: (Lee et al., 2019) applied a multi-modal deep learning approach combining MRI imaging, genetic data, and clinical variables to predict Alzheimer's disease progression from mild cognitive impairment, demonstrating that multimodal fusion improved prediction accuracy over single-modality models. While not focused on mortality, their architecture (modality-specific encoders with late fusion) is methodologically transferable.

RELEVANT DATASETS:

- **MIMIC-IV**: The target dataset for this research question. MIMIC-IV is the latest iteration of the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care, containing de-identified structured EHR data (demographics, diagnoses, procedures, lab results, vitals, medications, ICU flowsheets), clinical notes (discharge summaries, nursing notes, radiology reports), and linked imaging data (chest X-rays via MIMIC-CXR) for patients admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. It is the most comprehensive publicly available critical care dataset and the only one that natively supports trimodal (imaging + structured EHR + notes) mortality prediction research under a standardized protocol.

- **MIMIC-III benchmarks (Harutyunyan et al., 2019)**: Provided standardized train/validation/test splits and task definitions for mortality prediction and other clinical prediction tasks on MIMIC-III. These benchmarks established the experimental protocol conventions (cohort selection criteria, prediction windows, feature extraction pipelines) that a MIMIC-IV study should adapt and extend, ensuring comparability with prior results.

- **MIMIC-CXR**: The chest radiograph subset linked to MIMIC-IV, containing over 370,000 chest X-ray images with associated radiology reports. This is the imaging modality most naturally integrated with MIMIC-IV structured data and clinical notes for multimodal mortality prediction in the ICU setting.

- **EHR-derived structured and unstructured datasets (Zhang et al., 2020; Yadav et al., 2018)**: Multiple papers reviewed the use of EHR datasets containing both structured fields (lab values, demographics, medications, ICD codes) and unstructured clinical narratives for predictive modeling. (Yadav et al., 2018) provided a comprehensive overview of EHR data types and mining approaches, establishing that combining these data types consistently improves predictive performance across various clinical tasks.

PRIOR QUANTITATIVE RESULTS:

- **Unimodal structured EHR mortality prediction baselines**: (Harutyunyan et al., 2019) established that LSTM-based models on structured MIMIC-III time series data achieved AUROC of approximately 0.842–0.869 for in-hospital mortality prediction using their standardized benchmark splits. These values serve as the primary unimodal structured-data baseline that multimodal models must surpass.

- **Multimodal fusion improvements over unimodal models**: (Huang et al., 2020) reported across their systematic review that studies fusing medical imaging with EHR data generally observed improvements in AUROC of 2–8 percentage points over imaging-only or EHR-only models, though the magnitude varied substantially by task, dataset, and fusion strategy. Critically, they noted that most studies did not use standardized evaluation protocols, making cross-study comparison unreliable.

- **Structured + unstructured EHR fusion gains**: (Zhang et al., 2020) demonstrated that combining structured EHR variables with clinical notes in a deep learning framework yielded improvements in AUROC of approximately 3–5 percentage points over structured-data-only models for clinical outcome prediction tasks, with the clinical notes providing complementary information about physician assessments and symptom descriptions not captured in structured fields.

- **Multimodal ML in health outcomes**: (Kline et al., 2022) found in their scoping review that multimodal machine learning models in precision health consistently outperformed unimodal counterparts across a range of prediction tasks, but noted that the majority of studies used heterogeneous evaluation metrics, different data splits, and lacked statistical significance testing between modality configurations, limiting the strength of conclusions.

- **Class imbalance and metric selection for mortality**: (McDermott et al., 2024) demonstrated that for mortality prediction tasks with typical prevalence rates of 5–15%, AUROC and AUPRC can yield divergent model rankings. They showed that a model with higher AUROC may not have higher AUPRC, and recommended that studies report both metrics and use appropriate statistical tests (e.g., DeLong test for AUROC, bootstrap confidence intervals for AUPRC) to establish significance — directly informing the evaluation protocol requirements for this research question.

- **NLP extraction performance from clinical notes**: (Jagannatha & Yu, 2016) reported that RNN-based structured prediction models achieved F1 improvements of 3–7 percentage points over CRF baselines for clinical entity extraction, establishing that deep learning NLP encoders can extract richer representations from clinical notes than traditional methods, which in turn provides higher-quality input for multimodal fusion.

WHAT THIS QUESTION STILL NEEDS:

The existing literature reveals a significant gap between the demonstrated potential of multimodal fusion and the absence of a rigorous, controlled evaluation specifically addressing trimodal (imaging + structured EHR + clinical notes) mortality prediction on MIMIC-IV with a standardized protocol. While (Huang et al., 2020) and (Kline et al., 2022) both document that multimodal models tend to outperform unimodal baselines, both reviews emphasize that the evidence base is fragmented: studies use different datasets, different patient cohorts, different prediction windows, different preprocessing pipelines, and different evaluation metrics, making it impossible to attribute performance gains specifically to the addition of modalities rather than to confounding experimental design choices. No study in the provided corpus has conducted a trimodal fusion experiment on MIMIC-IV that simultaneously incorporates chest X-ray imaging, structured EHR time series, and clinical notes under a single standardized protocol with appropriate statistical testing between all modality configurations.

The methodological gaps are threefold. First, there is no established standardized preprocessing pipeline that covers all three modalities within MIMIC-IV in a unified manner — while FIDDLE (Tang et al., 2020) addresses structured EHR data and the MIMIC-III benchmarks (Harutyunyan et al., 2019) provide standardized splits for structured time series, neither extends to imaging or clinical notes, leaving critical decisions about image preprocessing, note encoding, temporal alignment across modalities, and missing modality handling unresolved. Second, prior multimodal studies have largely failed to perform rigorous statistical significance testing between modality ablations; most report point estimates of AUROC without confidence intervals or pairwise tests, which (McDermott et al., 2024) demonstrate is essential given the class-imbalanced nature of mortality prediction. Third, the question of whether each additional modality provides marginal predictive value — imaging on top of structured EHR, notes on top of structured EHR, and all three together versus any two — has not been systematically answered with appropriate ablation analysis and statistical correction for multiple comparisons.

A new study must therefore: (1) define a reproducible cohort selection and prediction window on MIMIC-IV (adapting the Harutyunyan benchmark protocol); (2) implement a standardized preprocessing pipeline for all three modalities; (3) train optimized unimodal baselines (imaging-only, structured-only, notes-only) and all pairwise and full trimodal combinations using a consistent architecture and hyperparameter search; (4) evaluate using both AUROC and AUPRC with bootstrap confidence intervals and DeLong or McNemar tests for pairwise comparisons; and (5) report effect sizes and statistical significance for every modality addition. Only such a controlled study can definitively answer whether trimodal fusion yields statistically significant improvements in in-hospital mortality prediction on MIMIC-IV, and which modality combinations drive the gains.
