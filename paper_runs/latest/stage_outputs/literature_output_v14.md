SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature clusters around several interconnected themes relevant to understanding Beethoven's piano sonatas, though remarkably few studies address the early-versus-late distinction directly. A substantial body of work examines sonata-form theory and its analytical frameworks, including Hepokoski and Darcy's normative sonata theory as applied to Haydn (Neuwirth 2011; Riley 2015), Caplin's functional theory extended to recapitulatory strategies, and investigations of cyclic integration across multi-movement cycles in Classical-era repertoire (Proksch 2019). These studies establish the formal vocabulary and structural expectations against which Beethoven's innovations—particularly his departure from normative practices in later works—can be measured, yet they treat Beethoven primarily as a reference point rather than as the central object of comparative inquiry. Performance practice scholarship has engaged more directly with Beethoven's sonatas, notably through debates over tempo indications (Noorduin 2016) and the re-examination of Czerny's and Moscheles's metronome marks (Noorduin 2017), as well as broader discussions of how performers interpret historical evidence (Bowen 1996). These contributions illuminate the practical dimensions of how Beethoven's intentions may have shifted across his career, but they do not systematically contrast the performance demands or interpretive challenges specific to early versus late sonatas.

A second cluster of research employs computational and statistical methods to model style evolution in Western classical music. Weiß, Mauch, and Dixon (2018) propose corpus-driven strategies for studying continuous stylistic change, while Georges (2017) and Georges and Nguyen (2019) use clustering, multi-dimensional scaling, and similarity matrices to visualize relationships among hundreds of composers across periods. These approaches demonstrate the feasibility of quantifying stylistic differentiation at scale, yet they operate at the level of composer-to-composer comparison or broad period classification rather than intra-composer evolution within a single genre. Related work on tonal practices—including directional tonality (Wadsworth 2012), common-tone tonality in Italian opera (Rothstein 2008), modal idioms in Rachmaninoff (Johnston 2014), and the affective properties of keys (Ishiguro 2023)—provides analytical tools for understanding harmonic innovation, but none of these studies apply their methodologies specifically to tracing Beethoven's harmonic evolution from his early to late sonatas.

A third, smaller thread concerns music cognition and expectation. Narmour (2000) develops rule-mapping models of melodic expectation, Bourne (2016) investigates the perception of musical irony through contextual inappropriateness, and Moorer (1972) explores early computational simulation of composition. These cognitive and computational perspectives offer potential avenues for understanding how listeners perceive stylistic change, but they have not been brought to bear on the specific question of what cognitively or perceptually distinguishes early from late Beethoven sonatas. Across all three clusters, the literature reveals a field rich in analytical frameworks, performance scholarship, and computational tools, yet conspicuously lacking in any integrative study that directly and systematically compares Beethoven's early and late piano sonatas along formal, harmonic, and stylistic dimensions.

GAPS:
1. No existing study provides a systematic, side-by-side comparative analysis of early versus late Beethoven piano sonatas using a unified analytical framework that integrates formal structure, harmonic language, and textural features within a single investigation.
2. Computational and corpus-based methods for style analysis—such as those demonstrated by Weiß et al. (2018) and Georges (2017)—have never been applied specifically to modeling intra-composer stylistic evolution across Beethoven's early, middle, and late sonata periods, despite their demonstrated capacity to quantify stylistic differentiation at scale.
3. The evolution of Beethoven's harmonic language from early to late sonatas—including his use of tonal pairing, chromatic mediants, modal mixture, and key relationships across movements—has not been studied with the systematic, corpus-driven rigor now available through music information retrieval tools, leaving a significant gap between the rich descriptive insights of traditional Schenkerian or neo-Riemannian analysis and the quantitative potential of computational harmony extraction.
4. While Noorduin (2016, 2017) has advanced understanding of Beethoven's tempo indications and posthumous metronome marks, no study has examined whether and how performance-related features—tempo flexibility, dynamic range, articulation density, textural complexity—differ systematically between early and late sonatas, either in the score or in recorded performance traditions.
5. The cognitive and perceptual dimensions of how listeners distinguish early from late Beethoven sonatas remain entirely unexplored; existing work on musical expectation (Narmour 2000), irony perception (Bourne 2016), and key affect (Ishiguro 2023) has not been applied to test whether listeners can reliably identify period membership or which musical features drive such judgments.
6. Cyclic integration—the inter-movement relationships within multi-movement works—is acknowledged as a hallmark of Beethoven's later style (Proksch 2019), but no study has quantitatively or systematically compared the degree and nature of cyclic integration in early versus late sonatas to determine whether this represents a gradual evolution or a sharp stylistic break.
7. The relationship between Beethoven's evolving piano technique, the changing physical capabilities of the instrument (as noted by Levinson and Balkin 1991 regarding the piano's developmental state), and the compositional demands placed on performers in early versus late sonatas has not been investigated through the lens of embodied music cognition or performance studies methodologies.
8. Existing sonata-form typologies and deformation theories (Hepokoski/Darcy, Caplin) have been applied extensively to Haydn and Mozart but have not been systematically deployed to classify and compare the formal strategies—particularly formal deformations, recapitulatory innovations, and developmental expansions—across the full trajectory of Beethoven's thirty-two sonatas.
