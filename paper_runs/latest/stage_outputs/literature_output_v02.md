SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature on AI in the stock market reveals three dominant thematic clusters. The first and most directly relevant cluster concerns the application of machine learning and deep learning methods to financial prediction tasks. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) demonstrated that machine learning methods—particularly tree-based models and neural networks—can substantially outperform traditional regression-based approaches in measuring asset risk premiums, in some cases doubling predictive performance. Bao, Yue, and Rao (2017) proposed a hybrid deep learning architecture combining wavelet transforms, stacked autoencoders, and LSTM networks for stock price forecasting, while Adebiyi, Adewumi, and Ayo (2014) established the superiority of artificial neural networks over ARIMA models for stock price prediction using NYSE data. Makridakis, Spiliotis, and Assimakopoulos (2018) provided a broader comparative evaluation of statistical versus machine learning forecasting methods across 1,045 time series, raising important questions about computational cost-performance tradeoffs. These works collectively establish that ML/DL methods hold significant promise for financial forecasting, though debates persist regarding optimal model selection and generalizability.

The second cluster encompasses sentiment analysis and its potential linkage to financial markets. Several papers provide foundational methods for extracting sentiment from social media and textual data: SemEval-2017 Task 4 (Rosenthal et al., 2017) advanced sentiment analysis on Twitter, Schouten and Frăsincar (2015) surveyed aspect-level sentiment analysis techniques, and Kralj Novak et al. (2015) explored the emotional semantics of emojis. Schwartz et al. (2013) demonstrated that open-vocabulary analysis of social media language can reveal personality and demographic traits. While these methods are clearly applicable to stock market sentiment analysis, notably few of the retrieved papers directly integrate advanced NLP-based sentiment signals into financial prediction models, suggesting a disconnect between the sentiment analysis community and the financial ML community.

The third cluster provides foundational finance theory and broader AI context. Shiller (2003) traced the evolution from efficient markets theory to behavioral finance, establishing the intellectual backdrop against which AI-driven market prediction operates. Harvey, Liu, and Zhu (2015) addressed the multiple-testing problem in asset pricing research, proposing higher statistical hurdles given extensive data mining—a concern directly relevant to ML-based financial research. Ramelli and Wagner (2020) examined stock price reactions to COVID-19, illustrating how exogenous shocks drive firm value through corporate debt and cash channels. The broader AI literature (Dwivedi et al., 2019; Acemoğlu & Restrepo, 2018; Brynjolfsson et al., 2017) discusses AI's transformative potential, labor market impacts, and the productivity paradox, while domain-specific surveys in healthcare (Bajwa et al., 2021), education (Zhai et al., 2021), and manufacturing (Cioffi et al., 2020) illustrate AI's cross-sector penetration—but notably, no comparable comprehensive survey exists specifically for AI in stock market applications.

GAPS:
1. No comprehensive, up-to-date survey paper exists that systematically maps the full landscape of AI techniques applied specifically to stock market tasks (prediction, portfolio optimization, risk management, trade execution), synthesizing methods, datasets, and performance benchmarks across studies.
2. The integration of advanced NLP methods—particularly transformer-based models such as BERT, GPT, and domain-specific financial language models (e.g., FinBERT)—with stock market prediction remains severely underexplored in the retrieved literature, despite the existence of mature sentiment analysis techniques.
3. There is a notable absence of research examining how AI model performance in stock prediction varies across different market regimes (bull vs. bear markets, high- vs. low-volatility periods, crisis vs. normal conditions), with most studies evaluating models on contiguous historical periods without regime-conditional analysis.
4. The literature lacks systematic studies combining alternative data sources (satellite imagery, credit card transaction data, web traffic, supply chain data, ESG metrics) with traditional price-volume data in unified deep learning architectures for stock market prediction.
5. Geographic and market-development bias is evident: nearly all empirical studies use data from developed markets (primarily NYSE), with emerging and frontier markets—where market microstructure, liquidity, and information asymmetry differ substantially—being almost entirely absent.
6. The critical disconnect between the sentiment analysis literature and the financial prediction literature represents an unresolved integration gap: sentiment methods are well-developed in isolation, but rigorous pipelines connecting real-time, multi-source social media sentiment to actionable stock market signals remain underdeveloped.
7. Explainability and interpretability of AI/ML models in stock market contexts is a major gap: while trustworthy AI frameworks exist (Díaz-Rodríguez et al., 2023), their application to financial prediction—where regulatory compliance, investor trust, and model auditability are paramount—has received minimal attention.
8. The ethical and systemic-risk implications of widespread AI adoption in stock markets—including algorithmic herding behavior, flash crash amplification, market fairness, and the potential for AI-driven manipulation—are virtually absent from the technical AI-in-finance literature.
9. There is insufficient research on the practical deployment challenges of AI stock prediction systems, including transaction cost modeling, slippage, market impact of AI-generated trades, and the gap between backtested performance and live trading performance.
10. The interaction between AI-driven trading and established finance theories (efficient market hypothesis, behavioral biases, factor models) remains undertheorized: few papers examine whether AI profits represent genuine alpha discovery, compensation for hidden risk factors, or merely sophisticated data mining—directly echoing Harvey, Liu, and Zhu's (2015) concerns about multiple testing.
