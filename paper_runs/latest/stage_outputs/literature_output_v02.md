SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature clusters around several interrelated domains that, while not directly addressing the specific comparison of Drake and Kendrick Lamar's influence on white teenagers, provide foundational context for understanding how hip-hop culture, celebrity musicians, and media shape adolescent attitudes and identity. A substantial body of work examines hip-hop's role in identity construction and cultural transmission among youth. (Evans, 2019) demonstrates that hip-hop-based education empowers youth through identity building and critical self-expression, while (Cohen, 2009) explores how rap music enables South African youth to enact and display social identities in daily life. (Chesley, 2011) provides evidence that non-African-American young adults can acquire African-American English vocabulary through hip-hop listening, suggesting that the genre functions as a vehicle for cross-cultural linguistic transmission. Extending this to digital spaces, (Cherjovsky, 2010) and (Johnson & Schell-Busey, 2016) document how hip-hop culture is experienced, negotiated, and contested in online communities, including through rap battle videos on YouTube.

A second major theme concerns the intersection of race, gender, and representation within hip-hop media. (Robillard, 2012) and (Lewis, 2010) analyze how music videos depict African-American women through sexualized and subordinate lenses, with (Lewis, 2010) specifically comparing portrayals across Caucasian and African-American hip-hop artists. (Bailey, 2018) introduces the concept of misogynoir to describe the anti-Black racist misogyny experienced by Black women, a framework relevant to understanding the gendered racial dynamics embedded in hip-hop culture. (Zichermann, 2013) investigates the effects of rap on female listeners in academic settings, while (Palma-Martos et al., 2021) documents a recent increase in female hip-hop consumption, suggesting shifting gender dynamics within the genre's audience. (Kågesten et al., 2016) and (Kenneavy, 2019) contribute broader evidence that early adolescence is a critical period for gender attitude formation, with media playing a significant role in shaping these attitudes.

A third theme addresses celebrity influence and media consumption patterns among young people. (Rojek, 2012) theorizes how celebrity culture permeates popular consciousness and shapes social and psychological outcomes, while (Moraes, 2016) examines how celebrity human brands influence consumer aspirations and behavior. (Eldik et al., 2019) finds that local social media influencers in diverse cities serve as role models for adolescent identity construction, and (Hall, 2005) demonstrates that personality characteristics predict media genre preferences. (Sayers, 2014) proposes a framework for understanding how mass media drives linguistic innovation across distant populations, a concept directly applicable to how hip-hop artists may influence language and cultural norms among demographically distant audiences, including white teenagers.

GAPS:
1. No study in this corpus directly examines the comparative influence of Drake and Kendrick Lamar on any demographic group, let alone white teenagers specifically. The existing literature treats hip-hop artists as a monolithic category rather than investigating how individual artists with distinct lyrical themes, public personas, and cultural positioning produce differential effects on listeners.
2. White teenagers as a distinct audience for hip-hop are almost entirely absent from the retrieved literature. While (Chesley, 2011) examines non-African-American young adults' acquisition of AAE vocabulary from hip-hop, and (Lewis, 2010) analyzes Caucasian artists' music videos, no study focuses on how white adolescents specifically receive, interpret, or are influenced by hip-hop artists, leaving a significant demographic gap.
3. There is no research examining the differential effects of hip-hop artists who represent contrasting cultural positions — such as Drake's commercially oriented, emotionally vulnerable persona versus Kendrick Lamar's politically conscious, socially critical approach — on the racial attitudes, cultural awareness, or identity development of white adolescent listeners.
4. The literature lacks longitudinal or experimental studies that measure changes in white teenagers' attitudes (regarding race, gender, masculinity, or cultural appropriation) as a function of exposure to specific hip-hop artists over time, with nearly all existing studies relying on cross-sectional or qualitative designs focused on other populations.
5. The role of streaming platforms and algorithmic curation in shaping white teenagers' exposure to and engagement with different hip-hop artists remains unexamined. While (Eldik et al., 2019) and (Johnson & Schell-Busey, 2016) address digital media, none investigate how platform-driven recommendation systems mediate the relationship between specific artists and adolescent audiences.
6. The potential mediating role of parasocial relationships — the one-sided emotional bonds fans form with celebrities — in shaping how white teenagers internalize messages from Drake or Kendrick Lamar is not addressed, despite (Moraes, 2016) and (Rojek, 2012) theorizing celebrity-audience relationships in general terms.
7. There is a notable absence of research examining how white teenagers' preexisting racial attitudes, socioeconomic backgrounds, or geographic contexts moderate the influence of hip-hop artists on their worldview, leaving open the question of whether hip-hop consumption reinforces, challenges, or has no effect on existing beliefs among this demographic.
