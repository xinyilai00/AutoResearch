SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature on gambling in teen mental health reveals a field that is surprisingly fragmented, with the majority of research addressing adjacent but distinct domains rather than the intersection of gambling and adolescent mental health directly. A substantial body of work documents the prevalence and correlates of behavioral addictions among adolescents, including pathological internet use (Durkee et al., 2012; Carli et al., 2012), online gaming addiction (Kuss & Griffiths, 2012; Teng et al., 2021), social networking site addiction (Kuss & Griffiths, 2011, 2017; Wu et al., 2013), and smartphone addiction (Matar Boumosleh & Jaalouk, 2017), with consistent findings linking these behaviors to depression, anxiety, and other psychopathology. Theoretical frameworks such as the I-PACE model (Brand et al., 2019) provide a generalizable account of how person-affect-cognition-execution interactions give rise to addictive behaviors, explicitly including gambling alongside gaming and other disorders. However, gambling-specific research within adolescent populations remains comparatively sparse, with Calado et al. (2016) offering one of the few systematic reviews of adolescent problem gambling prevalence, and Langham et al. (2015) proposing a comprehensive taxonomy of gambling-related harm that extends beyond disordered gambling to encompass broader community-level impacts.

A second major theme concerns digital mental health interventions for young people, including smartphone apps (Donker et al., 2013; Bakker et al., 2016), internet-based cognitive behavioral therapy (Ebert et al., 2015), and serious games or gamified interventions (Fleming et al., 2017; Garrido et al., 2019). These studies collectively demonstrate modest but promising efficacy for digital interventions targeting anxiety and depression in youth, though engagement and retention remain persistent challenges. Notably, none of these intervention studies specifically address gambling-related mental health problems in adolescents, representing a significant disconnect between the intervention literature and the identified prevalence of adolescent gambling problems.

A third theme encompasses broader contextual factors influencing adolescent mental health, including sedentary behavior (Hoare et al., 2016), the interplay of physical activity, screen time, and sleep (Sampasa-Kanyinga et al., 2020), substance abuse risk factors (Nawi et al., 2021), and the mental health impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic (Nochaiwong et al., 2021). While these studies provide important contextual grounding for understanding adolescent mental health determinants, they do not specifically incorporate gambling as a variable of interest, leaving its unique and interactive contributions to adolescent mental health burden largely unexamined within these broader ecological frameworks.

GAPS:
1. There is a critical absence of longitudinal studies tracking the developmental trajectory of gambling behavior and its causal relationship with mental health outcomes (depression, anxiety, suicidality) across adolescence, with nearly all existing evidence being cross-sectional and unable to establish temporal precedence or directionality.
2. The intersection of online and mobile gambling platforms with adolescent mental health is virtually unstudied, despite the rapid proliferation of mobile gambling applications and the known vulnerability of adolescents to digitally mediated addictive behaviors that have been extensively documented for gaming and social media.
3. No research examines the comorbidity and interaction effects between gambling and other behavioral addictions (gaming disorder, internet addiction, social media addiction) within adolescent populations, even though theoretical models like I-PACE suggest shared underlying mechanisms that may produce compounding mental health harm.
4. The impact of gambling-like mechanics in video games (loot boxes, skin betting, in-game purchases with randomized outcomes) on adolescent mental health is entirely absent from the retrieved literature, despite growing public health concern about these features blurring the boundary between gaming and gambling for young people.
5. There are no evidence-based prevention or early intervention programs specifically designed to address gambling-related mental health harm in adolescents, while digital intervention frameworks have been developed and tested for other youth mental health problems including anxiety, depression, and gaming disorder.
6. The role of gambling advertising exposure, particularly through social media and sports sponsorship, as a determinant of both gambling initiation and subsequent mental health outcomes among teenagers has not been investigated in any of the retrieved studies.
7. Cultural, socioeconomic, and gender-based moderators of the relationship between adolescent gambling and mental health are underexplored, with existing prevalence reviews providing limited disaggregated analysis of how these factors differentially shape vulnerability, harm severity, and help-seeking behavior.
8. No studies employ neurobiological or developmental neuroscience approaches to examine adolescent-specific vulnerability to gambling-related harm, despite established knowledge about ongoing prefrontal cortex maturation and reward system sensitivity during adolescence that may differentially predispose teens to gambling pathology compared to adults.
9. The family-level and peer-level social mechanisms through which adolescent gambling translates into mental health harm remain poorly understood, with existing harm taxonomies (Langham et al., 2015) describing impacts at a conceptual level but lacking empirical investigation of interpersonal transmission pathways specific to adolescent populations.
10. There is a methodological gap in the use of ecological momentary assessment (EMA) and experience sampling methods to capture real-time fluctuations in gambling urges, behavior, and mental health states among adolescents, with all retrieved studies relying on retrospective self-report measures subject to recall bias.
