RELEVANT METHODOLOGIES:
- **Computational tonality-based style analysis of classical music audio (Weiß, 2017 [16])**: This work develops and evaluates computational methods specifically for tonality-based style classification of classical music recordings. It represents the most directly relevant methodological precedent for the present question, as it addresses how algorithmic extraction of tonal and harmonic features from audio can be used to discriminate between stylistic periods within the classical canon. The approach involves feature extraction from audio representations followed by classification, establishing a pipeline directly analogous to what a Beethoven period-classification study would require.
- **Large-scale corpus aggregation of encoded functional harmonic analyses (Gotham, Micchi & Nápoles López, 2023 [15])**: The "When in Rome" meta-corpus methodology brings together over 2,000 human-made, computer-encoded functional harmonic analyses of 1,500 distinct works into a unified, machine-readable format. This methodology demonstrates how standardized symbolic harmonic annotations can be aggregated at scale to support tasks including machine learning for automatic analysis, composition, and classification—directly applicable to training a classifier on Beethoven's harmonic language across periods.
- **Quantitative modeling of mediant mixture and chromatic scale-degree usage (Temperley, Ren & Duan, 2017 [18])**: This study develops a computational and analytical framework for measuring mediant mixture—the simultaneous use of both natural and lowered scale degrees (e.g., 3̂ and ♭3̂)—in rock music. The methodology for quantifying mediant mixture frequency and modeling "blue notes" that fall between chromatic and diatonic degrees provides a directly transferable quantitative framework for measuring chromatic mediant frequency in Beethoven's harmonic vocabulary.
- **Tonal-chromatic system modeling for 19th-century music (Nicolette, 2015 [23])**: This work develops a formal model of the tonal-chromatic system and applies it to Mahler's works, addressing the interaction of diatonic tonal structures with chromatic harmonic entities. The modeling approach—formalizing how chromaticism interacts with underlying tonal frameworks in late-Romantic and extended-tonal music—provides a theoretical and computational scaffold for quantifying features like modulation distance and tonal pairing in Beethoven's transition from classical to late-style harmony.
- **Multi-structural harmonic analysis integrating functional, chromatic, and modal frameworks (Johnston, 2009 [17])**: This dissertation develops an interpretive framework for the interaction of functional tonal structures, equal-interval chromatic structures, and modal structures in Rachmaninoff's mature works. While applied to a later composer, the tripartite analytical framework—separating and then re-integrating functional, chromatic, and modal dimensions—offers a methodological template for decomposing Beethoven's harmonic language into the specific computable features named in the research question (modal mixture density, chromatic mediant frequency, tonal pairing).
- **Harmonic hierarchy and chord space modeling (Sallinen, 2010 [24])**: This work models harmonic hierarchy and chord space in popular music, providing a framework for representing harmonic relationships in a geometric or algebraic space. Such chord-space representations are relevant for computing modulation distance and for formalizing the notion of harmonic "distance" between keys or tonal regions in Beethoven's sonatas.

- **"When in Rome" Meta-corpus (Gotham et al., 2023 [15])**: Contains over 2,000 human-made, computer-encoded functional harmonic analyses of 1,500 distinct works spanning multiple periods and composers. This is the single most relevant existing dataset, as it provides standardized, machine-readable Roman numeral and functional harmonic annotations at scale. If Beethoven piano sonatas are included or can be added, this corpus provides the annotation infrastructure and format standards needed for the proposed study. Its scale and variety make it suitable for machine learning tasks including period classification.
- **Beethoven Piano Sonata scores and symbolic encodings (implied by [15] and [16])**: While no single Beethoven-specific dataset is explicitly named in the retrieved papers, the research question requires symbolic (MIDI, MusicXML, or Kern) encodings of all 32 Beethoven piano sonatas. The "When in Rome" corpus likely contains at least partial coverage of Beethoven sonatas given its scope. Additionally, publicly available corpora such as the MuseData Beethoven collection or the Kern Scores library provide the necessary symbolic score data from which harmonic features can be computationally extracted.
- **Classical music audio recordings with tonality annotations (Weiß, 2017 [16])**: The dataset underlying Weiß's computational style analysis work consists of classical music audio recordings paired with tonal and stylistic metadata. This demonstrates the feasibility of working with either audio-derived or score-derived representations for period classification tasks in classical music.

PRIOR QUANTITATIVE RESULTS:
- **Mediant mixture quantification in rock (Temperley et al., 2017 [18])**: This study provides quantitative measurements of the frequency of mediant mixture (co-occurrence of 3̂ and ♭3̂) across a corpus of rock songs, demonstrating that mediant mixture is extensive and systematic in rock. While the genre differs, the study establishes that mediant-related features can be operationalized as measurable, continuous variables suitable for statistical comparison—a critical methodological precedent for measuring chromatic mediant frequency in Beethoven.
- **Computational style classification accuracy for classical music (Weiß, 2017 [16])**: Weiß's work demonstrates that tonality-based computational features extracted from classical music audio can achieve meaningful discrimination between stylistic categories. The specific classification accuracies and feature importance rankings are not fully detailed in the abstract, but the work establishes that computational harmonic features carry sufficient signal for period/style classification in classical music, validating the core premise of the proposed research question.
- **Scale of encoded harmonic data for ML tasks (Gotham et al., 2023 [15])**: The "When in Rome" corpus demonstrates that aggregating over 2,000 encoded harmonic analyses across 1,500 works produces a dataset of sufficient scale and diversity for machine learning tasks including classification. This establishes a quantitative baseline: classification studies in computational musicology now operate at the scale of thousands of analyses, and any study of 32 Beethoven sonatas must address the small-sample challenge relative to this new standard.
- **Tonal-chromatic interaction modeling (Nicolette, 2015 [23]; Johnston, 2009 [17])**: Both works provide qualitative-to-semi-quantitative characterizations of how chromatic and modal harmonic devices increase in density and structural importance in later works of their respective composers (Mahler and Rachmaninoff). While not providing exact effect sizes for Beethoven, these studies establish the analytical expectation that later-period works in a composer's output tend to exhibit higher chromatic density, more frequent modal mixture, and greater modulation distance—hypotheses that the proposed study would test quantitatively for Beethoven.

WHAT THIS QUESTION STILL NEEDS:

The retrieved literature reveals a significant gap at the intersection of computational harmonic analysis and Beethoven period scholarship. While individual components exist—Weiß (2017) demonstrates computational tonality-based style classification for classical music generally, Gotham et al. (2023) provide a large-scale encoded harmonic corpus, and Temperley et al. (2017) operationalize mediant mixture as a quantifiable feature—no study has combined these elements into a unified, feature-engineered classification study specifically targeting the early-versus-late Beethoven sonata divide using the specific harmonic features named in this research question (chromatic mediant frequency, modal mixture density, tonal pairing instances, and modulation distance). The existing musicological literature on Beethoven's stylistic periods (e.g., the traditional three-period model) is overwhelmingly qualitative and hermeneutic, while the existing computational literature either addresses different composers, different feature sets, or different genres. A new study must bridge this gap directly.

Methodologically, the next study must address several specific challenges. First, it must define precise, reproducible computational operationalizations of each named feature: chromatic mediant frequency must be computed from symbolic score data (not audio, to preserve pitch-class precision), modal mixture density must be formalized as a ratio of borrowed chords to total harmonic events per movement, tonal pairing instances must be identified through a formalized algorithm (likely building on Nicolette's tonal-chromatic model), and modulation distance must be computed using a defined key-distance metric (e.g., steps on the circle of fifths or Schoenbergian distance). Second, with only 32 sonatas (approximately 10 early and 5 late, depending on periodization), the study faces a severe small-sample problem relative to the feature dimensionality; it must employ appropriate regularization, cross-validation, or dimensionality reduction strategies, and report feature importance rankings (e.g., via random forest feature importance, SHAP values, or logistic regression coefficients) to answer the second part of the question regarding which features contribute most to classification. Third, the study must decide whether to analyze at the sonata level, movement level, or sectional level, as this granularity choice fundamentally affects statistical power and interpretability.

Finally, the study must position its findings relative to the musicological consensus. The traditional three-period model (early, middle, late) is itself contested—some scholars argue for continuity rather than rupture between periods, and the boundary between "early" and "middle" sonatas (Opp. 2–28 as defined in this question actually spans what many theorists call both early and middle periods) is not universally agreed upon. A well-designed computational study should not merely assume the period labels but should test whether the harmonic features recover the traditional periodization or suggest alternative groupings. The study should also compare Beethoven's intra-composer harmonic trajectory against the patterns documented for other composers (e.g., the increasing chromatic density in late Mahler per Nicolette, or the modal-chromatic-functional interaction in late Rachmaninoff per Johnston) to determine whether Beethoven's late-style harmonic innovation is quantitatively exceptional or follows a common developmental arc. No existing study in the retrieved literature accomplishes any of this, making the proposed research both novel and necessary.
