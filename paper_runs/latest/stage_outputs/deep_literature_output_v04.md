RELEVANT METHODOLOGIES:
- **Computational tonality-based style analysis of classical music audio (Weiß, 2017)**: Directly addressed computational differentiation of classical music styles using tonality-based features extracted from audio recordings, including key detection, key clarity, modulation patterns, and harmonic progression analysis. This is the most methodologically central work for the key signature dimension of this question, as it provides algorithms specifically designed to quantify tonal characteristics that vary systematically across Baroque, Classical, Romantic, and contemporary styles. Key signature distribution, modulation frequency, and tonal stability metrics extracted by this pipeline are directly relevant to arousal regulation hypotheses, as tonal clarity and predictability are theorized to modulate cognitive load and emotional regulation in listener populations.
- **Pitch class profile analysis and circular pitch representation (Purwins, 2005)**: Developed computational models of pitch perception using toroidal representations of the 24 major/minor keys, converging psychoacoustic experiments with geometric music theory and computer simulations. This methodology provides a formal framework for quantifying key relationships, key distances in modulation, and the degree of tonal centricity in a given piece — all of which are critical for characterizing how key signature usage differs across classical sub-genres. The circular pitch model enables measurement of how far a piece deviates from a stable tonal center, which is directly relevant to cognitive engagement: pieces with frequent distant modulations (common in Romantic repertoire) impose different processing demands than those with stable tonic-dominant relationships (characteristic of Baroque music).
- **Automatic music genre classification using audio descriptors (Guaus, 2009)**: Developed a computational pipeline for extracting low-level and high-level audio features (spectral, temporal, rhythmic descriptors) from music signals and feeding them into classifiers, grounded in musicological and perceptual theories. While not focused on key signatures per se, this MIR framework provides the infrastructure for integrating key-related features (extracted via tonality analysis modules) with other acoustic descriptors in a unified classification pipeline, enabling multi-dimensional profiling of sub-genres.
- **Hierarchical MIDI-based genre classification with hybrid classifiers (McKay, 2004)**: Built a software system that classifies MIDI files into hierarchically organized taxonomies of musical genres using an extensive library of high-level musical features, including harmonic features such as key distribution, chord progression patterns, and modulation behavior. The harmonic feature library compiled in this work includes key-signature-related descriptors that could be directly extracted and compared across classical sub-genres, and the hybrid classification system demonstrates that sub-genre-level distinctions based on harmonic and tonal features are computationally tractable.
- **Neuroaesthetic study of atonal vs. tonal music perception (Mencke et al., 2019)**: Investigated how atonal music (characterized by absence of tonal hierarchical organization, low consonance, no tonic reference) is processed compared to Western tonal music, using neuroaesthetic frameworks. This work is critical for the key signature dimension because it establishes that the presence versus absence of a clear tonal center — the most extreme form of key signature variation — produces measurably different cognitive and emotional responses. The gradient from stable Baroque tonality through increasingly chromatic Romantic harmony to the modal or pitch-limited frameworks of contemporary minimalism can be conceptualized as a continuum of tonal organization, with this study anchoring one end of that continuum.
- **Latent Profile Analysis for sub-genre taste differentiation (Siebrasse & Wald-Fuhrmann, 2023)**: Applied Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) to sub-genre liking patterns within classical music listeners (N = 2,086, with n = 1,749 who liked European classical music). Identified four to six distinct taste classes within each of five popular genres, demonstrating that meaningful sub-genre-level distinctions exist and can be statistically modeled. The LPA methodology is directly transferable to clustering acoustic profiles along key-signature and tonality dimensions, enabling identification of natural groupings of pieces based on their tonal characteristics.
- **Entropy-based comparative music analysis (Febres & Jaffé, 2017)**: Analyzed polyphonic music files using a "Fundamental Scale" that produces Minimal Entropy Description, creating a novel representational space for comparing music pieces. This information-theoretic approach offers a complementary perspective on key signature complexity: pieces in stable keys with limited chromaticism (typical of Baroque) would be expected to produce lower entropy descriptions than pieces with frequent modulation and chromatic pitch content (typical of late Romantic), providing an independent validation of tonality-based sub-genre distinctions.
- **Emotion-to-Music Mapping Atlas / EMMA database construction (Strauß et al., 2024)**: Built a systematically organized online database of emotionally evocative music excerpts, categorized according to taxonomies derived from general emotion models (basic emotions, affective circumplex), rated for perceived emotions with interrater agreement metrics. This provides a methodological template for mapping key-signature-related acoustic features to arousal/valence dimensions relevant to therapeutic applications, as major/minor mode and key clarity are among the most robust acoustic predictors of perceived emotional content.
- **Emotion-based analysis and classification of audio music (Panda, 2019)**: Developed computational methods for classifying music based on emotional content rather than genre labels, bridging acoustic feature extraction with affective response models. Relevant for constructing emotion-driven taxonomies that incorporate key signature as a predictive feature, given the well-established association between mode (major/minor) and emotional valence.
- **MIR-based sub-genre classification in video game music (Hwang et al., 2026)**: Extracted quantifiable musical features from VGM across identified game genres using systematic MIR approaches, including key, tempo, energy, and spectral features. The inclusion of key as a discriminating feature in this sub-genre classification task validates its utility as a differentiator even in non-classical domains, supporting its inclusion in a classical sub-genre acoustic profiling pipeline.

RELEVANT DATASETS:
- **Classical music audio recordings corpus for tonality-based style analysis (Weiß, 2017)**: Audio recordings of classical music specifically curated for computational style differentiation, likely spanning multiple historical periods with tonality-based annotations. This is the most directly relevant dataset for key signature analysis across sub-genres, as it was assembled specifically to test tonality-based classification of classical styles and would contain the raw audio necessary for extracting key signature distributions, modulation patterns, and tonal stability metrics across Baroque, Classical, Romantic, and contemporary periods.
- **EMMA (Emotion-to-Music Mapping Atlas) database (Strauß et al., 2024)**: A systematically organized online database of emotionally evocative music excerpts with emotion ratings, interrater agreement values, and categorization according to affective circumplex and basic emotion models. Directly relevant as a source of annotated emotional music stimuli that could be cross-referenced with key signature metadata to test whether major/minor mode and key clarity predict arousal/valence ratings — a necessary step for linking key signature to therapeutic suitability.
- **MIDI genre classification corpus (McKay, 2004)**: A collection of MIDI files organized into hierarchical genre taxonomies, including classical sub-genres. MIDI representations are particularly well-suited for key signature extraction, as pitch class information is explicitly encoded, enabling reliable automated key detection, modulation tracking, and harmonic analysis without the noise and ambiguity of audio-based key estimation.
- **Representative German musical taste survey data (Siebrasse & Wald-Fuhrmann, 2023)**: Survey responses from N = 2,086 participants rating their liking of genres and sub-genres, including European classical music sub-genres. While perceptual rather than acoustic, this dataset provides validated sub-genre category labels that could ground a computational taxonomy and enable testing of whether key-signature-based acoustic clusters correspond to listener-perceived sub-genre boundaries.
- **Polyphonic music files for entropy analysis (Febres & Jaffé, 2017)**: Music files analyzed using the Fundamental Scale for entropy-based comparative analysis, providing a proof-of-concept dataset for information-theoretic characterization of musical complexity that could be cross-analyzed for key signature content to test the relationship between tonal organization and entropy metrics.

PRIOR QUANTITATIVE RESULTS:
- **Tonality-based style differentiation (Weiß, 2017)**: Developed and validated computational algorithms for extracting tonality-based features from classical music audio that can distinguish stylistic periods. The tonality feature set includes key detection confidence, key distribution entropy, modulation rate, and harmonic vocabulary characterization — all of which are directly interpretable in terms of key signature usage across sub-genres. Specific classification accuracies are not detailed in the abstract, but the work establishes that tonality-based features carry sufficient discriminative information to separate classical styles computationally.
- **Circular pitch and key relationship modeling (Purwins, 2005)**: Demonstrated convergence across psychoacoustic experiments, geometric music theory, and computer simulations in representing the 24 major/minor keys on a toroidal surface. This provides quantitative distance metrics between keys that can be used to measure modulation distance within and across sub-genres — for example, quantifying whether Baroque pieces modulate primarily to closely related keys (adjacent positions on the torus) while Romantic pieces exhibit more distant modulations, a hypothesis with direct implications for cognitive engagement and processing load.
- **Sub-genre taste classes within classical music (Siebrasse & Wald-Fuhrmann, 2023)**: Latent Profile Analysis identified four to six distinct taste classes within each of five genres (including European classical music) in a sample of N = 2,086 (with n = 1,749 classical music likers). This demonstrates that sub-genre-level differentiation is statistically meaningful and that broad genre labels mask substantial within-genre heterogeneity — heterogeneity that may be partly explained by key signature and tonal characteristics.
- **Atonal vs. tonal music processing differences (Mencke et al., 2019)**: Established that atonal music (lacking tonal hierarchy, consonance, and tonic reference) produces measurably different cognitive and emotional responses compared to tonal music. This provides an anchor point for the key signature continuum: if the complete absence of tonal organization produces distinct neurocognitive effects, then graded differences in tonal clarity — from the stable key centers of Baroque through the chromatic ambiguity of late Romantic to the modal frameworks of contemporary minimalism — may produce correspondingly graded effects on arousal regulation and cognitive engagement.
- **Entropy-based music characterization (Febres & Jaffé, 2017)**: Demonstrated that the Minimal Entropy Description scale provides a quantifiable, comparable metric across music pieces, enabling placement of pieces in a novel representational space. Showed that entropy content differs measurably across musical styles, providing a potential acoustic dimension for distinguishing sub-genres by structural complexity. Key signature stability is expected to correlate with lower entropy (fewer distinct pitch symbols needed in stable keys), while chromatic and modulating passages would increase entropy, making this metric a potential proxy for key-signature-related complexity.
- **Hybrid classification accuracy for hierarchical genre taxonomies (McKay, 2004)**: Achieved classification results using a hybrid system combining hierarchical, flat, and round-robin classification strategies on MIDI data with an extensive feature library including harmonic and key-related features, demonstrating that hierarchical sub-genre distinctions are computationally separable when tonality-informed features are included.
- **Key as a discriminating feature in VGM sub-genre classification (Hwang et al., 2026)**: Demonstrated that key, alongside tempo, energy, and spectral features, contributes to sub-genre-level differentiation in video game music, validating the inclusion of key signature as a meaningful feature in computational music taxonomies even outside the classical domain.

WHAT THIS QUESTION STILL NEEDS:

The retrieved literature provides a robust methodological foundation for extracting and analyzing key signature and tonality-related features from classical music — particularly through the tonality-based style analysis algorithms of Weiß (2017), the pitch class profile models of Purwins (2005), and the harmonic feature libraries of McKay (2004). However, a critical gap remains: no study has systematically mapped key signature characteristics (including mode distribution, key clarity, modulation frequency and distance, tonal stability, and chromatic density) across the four target sub-genres (Baroque, Classical period, Romantic, contemporary minimalism) and then linked these key-signature-derived dimensions to arousal regulation and cognitive engagement outcomes in dementia populations. The existing tonality-based classification work treats key signature as a feature for genre identification rather than as a therapeutically meaningful variable. The neuroaesthetic work of Mencke et al. (2019) establishes that tonal organization affects cognition and emotion, but has not been extended to the graded key-signature differences that distinguish Baroque stable tonality from Classical period balanced modulation, Romantic chromaticism, or contemporary minimalist modal frameworks. A new study must therefore construct a systematically sampled corpus across all four sub-genres, extract comprehensive key-signature and tonality features using the pipelines established by Weiß (2017) and Purwins (2005), and — critically — validate whether these features predict differential arousal regulation (measured via heart rate variability, skin conductance, or cortisol) and cognitive engagement (measured via attention duration, EEG markers, or behavioral responsiveness) in dementia patients.

Furthermore, the construction of a computational taxonomy of therapeutic suitability requires integrating key-signature-based acoustic profiles with clinical outcome data in a supervised or semi-supervised learning framework. The Latent Profile Analysis approach of Siebrasse and Wald-Fuhrmann (2023) could identify natural clusters of pieces based on their tonal and key-signature characteristics, but these clusters must be validated against therapeutic outcome measures to determine whether key-signature-based groupings correspond to meaningful differences in clinical efficacy. Specific hypotheses that remain untested include: whether the stable tonic-dominant key relationships and limited modulation of Baroque music produce more reliable arousal regulation in dementia patients than the frequent distant modulations of Romantic repertoire; whether the modal or pitch-limited frameworks of contemporary minimalism reduce cognitive overload relative to chromatically dense Romantic harmony; and whether major-mode pieces within each sub-genre produce more positive affective responses than minor-mode pieces, as would be predicted by general music-emotion theory but has not been tested in dementia populations with sub-genre specificity. A new study must be explicitly designed to test these hypotheses, producing both an open key-signature-annotated acoustic feature dataset and a validated predictive model that maps tonal and key-signature profiles to therapeutic suitability scores — a deliverable that is entirely absent from the current literature.
