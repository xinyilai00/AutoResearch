CANDIDATE RESEARCH QUESTIONS:
1. To what extent can computational harmonic analysis—extracting features such as chromatic mediant frequency, modal mixture density, tonal pairing instances, and modulation distance—quantitatively differentiate early Beethoven piano sonatas (Opp. 2–28) from late sonatas (Opp. 101–111), and which harmonic features contribute most to period classification? | Gap addressed: Gap 3 — the evolution of Beethoven's harmonic language has not been studied with systematic, corpus-driven computational rigor. | Feasibility: MusicXML and MIDI encodings of all 32 sonatas are publicly available from repositories such as MuseScore, the Music21 corpus, and the Essen Folksong/Europa corpus; harmonic feature extraction can be performed using music21, music21j, or MIR toolkits, and classification can be evaluated with standard machine learning pipelines on public data.

2. Can a multi-dimensional computational framework integrating formal structure annotations, harmonic vocabulary metrics, and textural feature vectors (voice count, registral spread, note density) reliably classify Beethoven's piano sonatas into early, middle, and late periods, and what is the relative discriminative power of each feature dimension? | Gap addressed: Gap 1 and Gap 2 — no unified analytical framework has been applied, and corpus-based style analysis methods have not been used for intra-composer evolution in Beethoven's sonatas. | Feasibility: Symbolic score data (MusicXML/MIDI) for all 32 sonatas are publicly available; formal structure annotations can be derived from published analytical literature or encoded manually; textural and harmonic features can be computed algorithmically; classification and feature importance can be assessed using standard supervised learning methods.

3. How does the degree of cyclic integration—measured through computational thematic similarity (motivic contour matching, intervallic profile correlation, and pitch-class set overlap) between movements within each sonata—differ between early and late Beethoven piano sonatas, and does the trajectory suggest gradual evolution or a sharp stylistic break? | Gap addressed: Gap 6 — cyclic integration has not been quantitatively compared across early and late sonatas. | Feasibility: Symbolic encodings of all movements are publicly available; motivic and contour similarity can be computed using music21 or custom Python implementations of established music-theoretic algorithms (e.g., contour adjacency, interval-class vectors); breakpoint analysis and time-series methods can test for gradual versus abrupt change.

4. How do score-prescribed performance features—specifically tempo marking flexibility (frequency and type of tempo modifications), dynamic range (number and variety of dynamic indications per movement), articulation density (articulation marks per measure), and textural complexity (simultaneous voice count and registral span)—differ systematically between early and late Beethoven piano sonatas as notated in the score? | Gap addressed: Gap 4 — no study has examined systematic differences in performance-related features between early and late sonatas in the score. | Feasibility: MusicXML scores encode tempo markings, dynamics, articulations, and voice assignments; these can be parsed and quantified programmatically from publicly available digital scores; statistical comparison across period groupings requires no proprietary data or human subjects.

5. To what extent do sonata-form deformation patterns—operationalized as deviations from Hepokoski/Darcy normative expectations in exposition structure, developmental scope (development-to-exposition length ratio), and recapitulatory strategy (exact, varied, or truncated)—differ in frequency and type between early and late Beethoven piano sonatas when classified using a consistent analytical protocol? | Gap addressed: Gap 8 — existing sonata-form typologies have not been systematically deployed across all 32 sonatas for comparative purposes. | Feasibility: Published analytical annotations of sonata-form sections for all 32 sonatas exist in the music-theoretic literature and can be encoded; structural measurements (section lengths, thematic counts, key areas) can be computed from symbolic scores; deformation classification can follow published taxonomies applied consistently across the corpus.

6. How does Beethoven's use of textural stratification—measured computationally as the number of simultaneous independent voice layers, registral gap frequency, and the incidence of contrapuntal versus homophonic textures—evolve across the early, middle, and late piano sonatas, and is this evolution correlated with the expanding range of the piano instrument during his lifetime? | Gap addressed: Gap 7 — the relationship between evolving piano capabilities and compositional textural demands has not been investigated computationally. | Feasibility: Symbolic score data encode voice and register information; textural features can be extracted algorithmically; historical data on piano range expansion (e.g., from organological literature and public museum catalogs) are available to correlate with compositional demands across the sonata timeline.

7. Can unsupervised clustering and dimensionality reduction techniques (e.g., t-SNE, UMAP, hierarchical clustering) applied to a high-dimensional feature space derived from Beethoven's piano sonatas recover the conventional early-middle-late periodization, and if so, which feature dimensions most strongly drive the clustering structure? | Gap addressed: Gap 2 — computational style analysis methods such as those by Weiß et al. (2018) and Georges (2017) have not been applied to intra-composer stylistic evolution within Beethoven's sonata output. | Feasibility: All required input data (symbolic scores) are publicly available; feature extraction can be performed with open-source MIR libraries; clustering and visualization methods are standard computational techniques requiring no proprietary tools or data.

8. How does the complexity of Beethoven's rhythmic vocabulary—measured through metrics such as rhythmic entropy, syncopation frequency, metric displacement incidence, and the diversity of durational patterns—change from early to late piano sonatas, and do late sonatas exhibit significantly greater rhythmic irregularity than early sonatas? | Gap addressed: Gap 1 — no unified comparative study has examined rhythmic dimensions alongside formal and harmonic features across early and late sonatas. | Feasibility: Rhythmic features can be extracted from publicly available MIDI and MusicXML files using music21 or similar libraries; entropy and diversity metrics are standard information-theoretic computations; statistical tests can compare distributions across period-defined groups without requiring any non-public resources.
