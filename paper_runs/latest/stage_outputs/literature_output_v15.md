SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature reveals a strikingly thin body of scholarship directly addressing the comparative study of early versus late Beethoven piano sonatas. Of the twenty-one papers surveyed, only a small subset engages with Beethoven's sonata output in any substantive way. Kwon (2008) examines Beethoven's two-movement piano sonatas within the broader context of eighteenth-century Italian keyboard traditions, situating Beethoven's structural choices against a lineage of predecessors but focusing on movement count rather than on a systematic early-versus-late stylistic comparison. Proverbio, Qin, and Milovanovič (2026) investigate the cognitive mapping of emotional cues in piano performance using excerpts drawn partly from Beethoven's works, attending to key, tempo, note density, and acoustic energy, yet their behavioral-experimental paradigm is oriented toward general music cognition rather than toward a musicological periodization of Beethoven's output. Burgstaller (2015) discusses Heinrich Schenker's analytical engagement with Beethoven—including insights into structural coherence across Beethoven's works—but frames this within Schenker's broader intellectual biography and his activities as a music journalist rather than as a dedicated early-late sonata comparison.

The remaining papers, while individually valuable, orbit the topic at considerable distance. Stroud (2019) offers a penetrating account of notational eccentricity and performative difficulty in Beethoven's late string quartets, providing a methodological template—close source-study paired with performance analysis—that has not yet been transferred to the piano sonatas. O'Hara (2017) theorizes recomposition as an analytical tool applicable to canonical repertoire, and Scanlon (2017) reimagines pianistic technique through the lens of left-hand repertoire, but neither addresses Beethoven's sonatas directly. Broader contextual studies on nineteenth-century performance culture (Crisp, 2024; Jacobson, 2020), reception history (Neill, 2014; Hester, 2015), and analytical theory (Kalinajova Schwarz, 2017) furnish useful intellectual background but do not intersect with the specific early-versus-late sonata question.

Collectively, the literature underscores that while Beethoven's sonatas individually—whether early or late—have attracted considerable musicological attention elsewhere, the retrieved corpus contains no sustained, dedicated comparative study that places early and late piano sonatas in systematic dialogue. The existing work tends either to treat the sonatas within a single period, to examine them through the lens of a single analytical parameter (e.g., movement structure, emotional perception), or to address adjacent Beethoven genres such as the string quartets.

GAPS:
1. No retrieved study undertakes a direct, systematic comparison of early and late Beethoven piano sonatas across multiple analytical dimensions (harmonic language, formal structure, textural complexity, thematic development) within a single unified framework.
2. The cognitive and perceptual dimensions of how listeners differentiate early from late Beethoven sonatas remain entirely unexamined; the one behavioral study retrieved (Proverbio et al., 2026) uses Beethoven excerpts but does not operationalize the early-late distinction as an independent variable.
3. Performance-practice and notation-based analysis, which Stroud (2019) has applied productively to Beethoven's late string quartets, has not been extended to a comparative study of notational and interpretive differences between early and late piano sonatas.
4. Computational or corpus-based musicological methods—such as algorithmic feature extraction, statistical stylometry, or machine-learning classification of stylistic periods—are entirely absent from the retrieved literature on Beethoven's sonatas.
5. The reception history of Beethoven's early versus late sonatas as distinct critical categories—how critics, theorists, and audiences from the nineteenth century to the present have constructed, reinforced, or challenged the early-late binary—has not been investigated in any of the retrieved sources.
6. Schenkerian and post-Schenkerian analytical frameworks, despite Schenker's well-documented engagement with Beethoven (Burgstaller, 2015), have not been systematically applied to a comparative early-late sonata study in the retrieved corpus.
7. The relationship between Beethoven's biographical trajectory (particularly his hearing loss and its progression) and the measurable stylistic divergence between early and late sonatas remains unaddressed in the retrieved literature, despite its obvious relevance.
8. Cross-genre comparison—examining whether the early-late stylistic shift observed in the piano sonatas parallels, precedes, or diverges from analogous shifts in Beethoven's string quartets, symphonies, or violin sonatas—is not represented among the retrieved papers.
