CANDIDATE RESEARCH QUESTIONS:
1. What is the cross-sectional association between daily coffee consumption frequency and validated depression and anxiety screening scores among adolescents aged 12-19 in the United States? | Gap addressed: Gap 1 — no retrieved study directly investigates coffee consumption and mental health outcomes specifically in adolescent populations. | Feasibility: NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) provides publicly available, nationally representative data including dietary intake questionnaires with coffee consumption frequency and validated mental health instruments (PHQ-9, GAD-7) for adolescents across multiple survey cycles, enabling robust epidemiological analysis with large sample sizes.

2. Does sleep duration and sleep quality statistically mediate the relationship between coffee consumption and depressive and anxiety symptoms in adolescents, and what proportion of the total effect is explained by the sleep pathway? | Gap addressed: Gap 3 — the mediating pathway from coffee consumption to adolescent mental health via sleep disruption is implied but has not been formally tested as a causal chain. | Feasibility: NHANES and the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study both contain concurrent measures of caffeine/beverage intake, sleep duration and quality (actigraphy in ABCD), and mental health outcomes, enabling formal mediation analysis using structural equation modeling with publicly accessible datasets.

3. What is the shape of the dose-response relationship between daily coffee intake (measured in cups per day and estimated milligrams of caffeine) and specific mental health symptom dimensions including anxiety sensitivity, anhedonia, and irritability in adolescents aged 12-19? | Gap addressed: Gap 9 — the dose-response relationship between coffee intake and specific mental health symptom dimensions in adolescents has not been characterized. | Feasibility: NHANES dietary recall data provides quantitative coffee intake, and mental health subscale scores can be derived from administered screening instruments; restricted cubic spline regression and generalized additive models can characterize nonlinear dose-response curves using these public data.

4. Does the association between coffee consumption and adolescent mental health outcomes differ significantly between males and females after adjusting for body weight, pubertal stage, and total caffeine exposure? | Gap addressed: Gap 8 — potential sex differences in the relationship between coffee consumption and adolescent mental health remain unexplored despite known sex differences in caffeine metabolism and disorder prevalence. | Feasibility: NHANES and Add Health (National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health) include sex, anthropometric measures, pubertal development markers, dietary caffeine intake, and mental health outcomes, enabling sex-stratified and interaction analyses with publicly available data.

5. Is there a statistically significant interaction between adolescent chronotype (morning-type versus evening-type) and coffee consumption in predicting sleep latency, sleep duration, and internalizing symptoms? | Gap addressed: Gap 6 — the interaction between coffee consumption and chronotype in adolescents, particularly compensatory use by evening types, has not been investigated. | Feasibility: The ABCD Study and several publicly available adolescent cohort datasets include chronotype assessments (Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire), caffeine intake measures, sleep parameters, and mental health scales, enabling interaction modeling with adequate sample sizes.

6. Does coffee consumption moderate the established associations between screen time, physical activity level, and academic stress with adolescent depression and anxiety symptoms? | Gap addressed: Gap 10 — no study examines coffee consumption as a confounding or moderating variable in established adolescent mental health risk factors. | Feasibility: Add Health and NHANES contain measures of screen time, physical activity, academic performance/stress, coffee and caffeine consumption, and mental health outcomes, enabling moderation and confounding analyses through multivariable regression and interaction terms.

7. What are the longitudinal trajectories of coffee consumption initiation and escalation during adolescence (ages 12-18), and do these trajectories predict internalizing disorder onset in young adulthood (ages 19-25)? | Gap addressed: Gap 7 — longitudinal data on adolescent coffee consumption patterns and subsequent mental health trajectories into young adulthood are entirely absent. | Feasibility: Add Health follows a nationally representative adolescent cohort across five waves from 1994 to 2018 with publicly available data on beverage consumption, mental health, and life-course outcomes, enabling growth mixture modeling and survival analysis for disorder onset.

8. Can a physiologically based pharmacokinetic model of caffeine metabolism, parameterized with age-specific hepatic enzyme activity and body composition data, predict differential caffeine exposure and half-life in adolescents versus adults, and what are the implications for safe intake thresholds? | Gap addressed: Gap 4 — existing caffeine safety reviews do not provide age-stratified analyses or adolescent-specific dosage thresholds for adverse mental health effects. | Feasibility: PBPK modeling is entirely computational; age-specific physiological parameters (CYP1A2 ontogeny, body water fraction, hepatic blood flow) are available from published pediatric pharmacology literature and public databases such as the EPA's Exposure Factors Handbook, enabling simulation-based derivation of adolescent-specific exposure profiles.

9. To what extent do the polyphenol and chlorogenic acid content of coffee, as distinct from its caffeine content, account for variation in anti-inflammatory biomarkers associated with depression risk in adolescents? | Gap addressed: Gap 5 — no study distinguishes the mental health effects of coffee as a whole beverage from those of isolated caffeine in adolescent populations. | Feasibility: NHANES includes dietary intake data enabling estimation of both caffeine and polyphenol exposure from coffee, alongside circulating inflammatory biomarkers (CRP, IL-6) measured in stored biospecimens; regression decomposition and mediation analysis can partition variance attributable to each component using public-use data files.
