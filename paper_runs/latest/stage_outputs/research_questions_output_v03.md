CANDIDATE RESEARCH QUESTIONS:
1. How do acoustic features (tempo, harmonic complexity, dynamic range, spectral centroid) differ across classical music sub-genres (Baroque, Classical, Romantic, Minimalist), and which feature profiles are most predictive of sustained attention enhancement based on computational modeling of arousal-attention relationships? | Gap addressed: Gap 3 — Sub-genre differences within classical music have not been investigated for differential effects on attention. | Feasibility: Public music databases (e.g., GTZAN, Million Song Dataset, IMSLP recordings) and music information retrieval libraries (librosa, Essentia) enable systematic extraction of acoustic features across sub-genres; computational models of arousal and attention (e.g., Yerkes-Dodson curve simulations) can predict attentional outcomes without human subjects.

2. Can a meta-analytic synthesis of published effect sizes resolve the contradictory findings on background music and cognitive performance when restricted to classical music stimuli, and what task-level and participant-level moderators explain the heterogeneity? | Gap addressed: Gap 9 — Contradictory findings on whether background music facilitates or impairs cognitive performance have not been resolved specifically for classical music. | Feasibility: Published effect sizes, tables, and supplementary data from existing studies are publicly accessible; standard meta-analytic methods (random-effects models, meta-regression) can be executed computationally using open-source statistical packages (R metafor, Python meta-analysis libraries) on extracted data.

3. What is the relative contribution of arousal modulation, mood enhancement, and neural entrainment as mechanistic pathways linking classical music listening to attentional performance, as estimated through an integrated computational causal model? | Gap addressed: Gap 4 — The mechanistic pathway by which classical music influences attention has not been tested in an integrated model. | Feasibility: Published effect sizes from isolated mechanism studies (dopamine, arousal-mood, predictive coding, entrainment) can be parameterized into a structural equation model or Bayesian causal network; sensitivity analysis and Monte Carlo simulation can estimate pathway contributions using only published data and computational tools.

4. To what extent do individual difference variables (musical sophistication, baseline attentional capacity, openness to experience, working memory span) moderate the effect of classical music on attention, as revealed by re-analysis of open-access experimental datasets? | Gap addressed: Gap 7 — Individual difference variables have not been systematically examined as moderators of classical music's effect on attention. | Feasibility: Open-access repositories (OSF, Zenodo, OpenNeuro, ICPSR) contain experimental datasets with individual difference measures and cognitive task performance under music conditions; secondary analysis with moderation regression and mixed-effects models is fully computational.

5. How do the cognitive load characteristics of classical music (measured via acoustic complexity indices, information density, and predictability metrics) compare to pop, ambient, and silence conditions, and do these characteristics predict direction and magnitude of attention effects across task types? | Gap addressed: Gaps 1 and 9 — Classical music has not been isolated as an independent variable for attention, and its cognitive load profile relative to other genres remains unexamined. | Feasibility: Large corpora of classical, pop, and ambient music are publicly available; computational music analysis (entropy measures, tonal complexity, rhythmic regularity indices) can quantify cognitive load characteristics, and these can be correlated with published attention effect sizes across task types.

6. Can analysis of publicly available neuroimaging and electrophysiological datasets reveal how classical music listening modulates attention-network activity (frontoparietal activation, theta/beta power ratios, default mode network suppression) compared to silence or non-classical music? | Gap addressed: Gap 10 — Neurophysiological correlates of attention during classical music listening have not been studied using modern methods. | Feasibility: Open neuroimaging repositories (OpenNeuro, HCP, EEGbase, LEMON dataset) contain resting-state and task-based EEG/fMRI data collected under music or auditory stimulation conditions; standardized preprocessing pipelines (MNE-Python, fMRIPrep) enable re-analysis focused on attention-network markers.

7. What dose-response relationship exists between cumulative classical music exposure (hours per week, years of engagement) and attentional capacity, as modeled through computational simulation calibrated against published cross-sectional musician vs. non-musician effect sizes? | Gap addressed: Gaps 2 and 6 — The dose-response relationship and longitudinal effects of classical music engagement on attention remain unexplored. | Feasibility: Published effect sizes comparing musicians and non-musicians on attention tasks provide calibration points; agent-based or dynamical systems models can simulate dose-response curves under varying assumptions about exposure duration, engagement mode (passive vs. active), and decay rates, all computationally.

8. Does classical music exposure differentially predict attention-related outcomes across cultural and age-group contexts, as assessed through cross-national analysis of publicly available education, music consumption, and cognitive assessment databases? | Gap addressed: Gap 5 — The literature is heavily skewed toward Western adult populations with no studies on children, adolescents, or cross-cultural variation. | Feasibility: International databases (PISA cognitive assessment data, UNESCO cultural participation surveys, World Values Survey) contain cross-national measures of music engagement and cognitive/educational outcomes; multilevel modeling can examine age and cultural moderation of music-attention associations using these public records.
