CANDIDATE RESEARCH QUESTIONS:
1. How does the predicted enamel demineralization risk from a simulated acute high-sugar exposure event (modeled on typical Halloween candy consumption patterns of 2-5 days) compare to the risk from equivalent total sugar distributed as chronic daily intake over 30 days? | Gap addressed: No research has compared the cariogenic risk profile of episodic high-sugar surges versus chronic daily sugar intake (Gap 2). | Feasibility: Established computational enamel demineralization models (e.g., Stephan curve pH dynamics, ICDAS-based caries risk algorithms) can simulate both consumption patterns using published sugar concentration, exposure frequency, and salivary buffering parameters from public literature, requiring no physical experiments.

2. To what extent do different candy types commonly distributed at Halloween (chocolate, gummy/sticky candies, hard candies, sour candies) differ in their predicted cariogenic and erosive potential based on computational modeling of sugar release kinetics, oral retention time, and acidogenic pH profiles? | Gap addressed: No differential assessment of Halloween candy types for cariogenic potential in pediatric populations (Gap 4). | Feasibility: Published data on candy composition, sugar content, pH values, and oral clearance times are available from USDA food databases and prior in-vitro studies; these parameters can feed into established caries risk simulation models to rank candy types without requiring new laboratory work.

3. Using publicly available national health survey data (e.g., NHANES), is there a detectable association between household socioeconomic status indicators and children's reported dental caries experience in the months following the October-November period, and does this seasonal signal differ from caries patterns observed in non-holiday months? | Gap addressed: The intersection of holiday candy consumption with socioeconomic disparities in oral health remains unexplored (Gap 6). | Feasibility: NHANES provides publicly accessible, nationally representative data on children's oral health examinations, household income, food security status, and dietary recall, with date-of-examination fields that enable seasonal analysis across multiple survey cycles.

4. Can analysis of publicly deposited oral microbiome sequencing datasets (e.g., from the Human Microbiome Project or Sequence Read Archive) reveal whether dietary sugar perturbation signatures in oral microbial communities are consistent with acute transient shifts versus sustained compositional changes, and what microbial taxa serve as indicators of recent high-sugar exposure? | Gap addressed: No study has used oral microbiome sequencing to track acute shifts during and after concentrated candy consumption (Gap 5). | Feasibility: Thousands of oral microbiome 16S rRNA and metagenomic samples are publicly available in NCBI SRA and HMP databases, including samples with dietary metadata; computational re-analysis can identify sugar-associated microbial signatures and temporal dynamics without new sample collection.

5. What is the predicted moderating effect of different post-consumption oral hygiene interventions (immediate brushing, delayed brushing at 30 or 60 minutes, water rinsing, no intervention) on enamel surface loss following a simulated concentrated candy consumption episode, as modeled through established dental erosion computational frameworks? | Gap addressed: No research on the moderating role of post-consumption oral hygiene behaviors specifically following concentrated candy-eating episodes (Gap 3). | Feasibility: Published enamel erosion models incorporating pH recovery curves, salivary flow rates, fluoride remineralization kinetics, and brushing abrasion coefficients can simulate each hygiene scenario computationally using parameters from existing literature.

6. Using synthetic cohort simulation methods, what are the projected 6-month and 12-month caries progression outcomes for children exposed to a single acute high-candy event (modeled on Halloween) under varying assumptions about baseline caries risk, fluoride exposure, and subsequent dietary patterns? | Gap addressed: No longitudinal study has tracked whether a single high-exposure candy event produces lasting effects on caries progression (Gap 8). | Feasibility: Established caries progression models (e.g., CAMBRA-based risk assessment, Markov chain caries state-transition models) can simulate longitudinal outcomes using published transition probabilities and risk factor weights, enabling projection of long-term effects from acute exposures without requiring multi-year human studies.

7. What is the quantified relationship between frequency of sugar exposure events per day and predicted cariogenic risk, as derived from meta-analytic synthesis of published dose-response data, and does this relationship exhibit a threshold or nonlinear acceleration effect at the high exposure frequencies typical of Halloween candy consumption? | Gap addressed: The temporal dynamics of episodic high-sugar exposure versus chronic intake remain unclear, and no study has characterized Halloween-level consumption patterns (Gaps 1 and 2). | Feasibility: Published epidemiological and experimental studies reporting sugar exposure frequency and caries outcomes are available for systematic extraction and dose-response meta-analysis using standard statistical methods, requiring only published literature as the data source.

8. Based on computational modeling of parental candy management strategies (complete restriction, rationing to fixed daily amounts, candy type substitution, buyback-and-remove programs), which strategy produces the lowest predicted cumulative cariogenic exposure over the 14-day post-Halloween period? | Gap addressed: No research on parental mediation strategies during Halloween and their effects on dental outcomes (Gap 7). | Feasibility: Each parental strategy can be parameterized as a sugar exposure schedule (frequency, duration, candy type, quantity) and evaluated through established caries risk simulation models using published oral environment parameters, enabling direct comparison without human subjects research.
