CANDIDATE RESEARCH QUESTIONS:
1. Can Transformer-based architectures (e.g., Informer, Autoformer, PatchTST) outperform LSTM and CNN-LSTM baselines in multi-horizon stock price forecasting when trained on publicly available OHLCV data from major indices? | Gap addressed: Transformer architectures remain largely unexplored for stock prediction (Gap 1) | Feasibility: Public OHLCV data is freely available via Yahoo Finance and similar APIs; Transformer implementations exist in open-source libraries (e.g., Time-Series-Library); standard forecasting metrics (RMSE, MAE, directional accuracy) enable quantitative comparison.

2. Does integrating sentiment-derived features extracted from publicly available financial news headlines and Reddit/Twitter posts improve the predictive accuracy of deep learning stock forecasting models compared to models using only technical indicators? | Gap addressed: No integration of behavioral finance theory with modern deep learning approaches (Gap 2) and alternative/unstructured data sources are not represented (Gap 6) | Feasibility: Public news corpora (e.g., Financial PhraseBank, Reuters headlines) and social media data (Reddit r/wallstreetbets, Twitter finance posts) are accessible; NLP sentiment models (FinBERT) are open-source; performance can be measured via standard backtesting frameworks.

3. Do ensemble strategies that combine heterogeneous deep learning architectures (e.g., stacking CNN-LSTM, Transformer, and gradient-boosted tree predictions) produce statistically significant improvements in risk-adjusted returns over individual constituent models for equity forecasting? | Gap addressed: Ensemble methods are surveyed in general but not systematically applied to stock prediction (Gap 4) | Feasibility: All constituent models have open-source implementations; public market data enables reproducible training; statistical significance of return differences can be tested via Diebold-Mariano tests and bootstrap methods on publicly available historical data.

4. Can post-hoc explainability methods (SHAP, Integrated Gradients, attention weight visualization) applied to deep learning stock prediction models identify feature attributions that are consistent with established financial theory, and do more interpretable models exhibit different out-of-sample performance profiles? | Gap addressed: Explainability and interpretability of AI models in financial contexts are entirely absent (Gap 3) | Feasibility: SHAP and Integrated Gradients are available as open-source Python libraries; models can be trained on public data and their explanations compared against known factor premiums (e.g., Fama-French factors); no proprietary data or human subjects required.

5. To what extent can modern deep learning models extract persistent, statistically significant alpha from publicly available market data after accounting for transaction costs, and does this evidence support or refute the adaptive markets hypothesis over the period 2010–2025? | Gap addressed: The tension between efficient markets theory and ML predictive capability lacks rigorous modern examination (Gap 5) | Feasibility: Historical price data is publicly available; transaction cost models can be parameterized from published exchange fee schedules; statistical tests for alpha persistence (e.g., t-tests on Sharpe ratios, bootstrap under the null of no predictability) are standard computational procedures.

6. Does transfer learning from models pre-trained on large, liquid markets (e.g., S&P 500 constituents) improve prediction accuracy on smaller or emerging market equities compared to training from scratch, and which transfer strategies (frozen backbone, fine-tuning, adapter layers) are most effective? | Gap addressed: Cross-market, multi-asset, and transfer learning approaches are unexplored (Gap 8) | Feasibility: Public market data from multiple exchanges (US, European, Asian) is freely accessible; transfer learning protocols can be implemented with standard deep learning frameworks; comparative experiments require only computational resources and public data.

7. How do deep learning stock prediction models degrade under market regime changes (e.g., the 2020 pandemic crash, the 2022 rate-hike cycle), and can online continual learning with concept drift detection mechanisms mitigate performance collapse compared to static offline-trained models? | Gap addressed: Real-time, online, and continual learning paradigms are absent (Gap 7) and model robustness and stress testing are unstudied (Gap 9) | Feasibility: Historical regime-change periods are well-documented and data is publicly available; online learning algorithms (e.g., adaptive windowing, drift detection methods) have open-source implementations; performance degradation can be quantified via rolling-window evaluation on historical data without requiring live deployment.

8. Which combination of technical indicators, fundamental ratios, and macroeconomic variables maximizes the predictive performance of deep learning stock models, and does the optimal feature set differ across model architectures (LSTM vs. Transformer vs. tree-based)? | Gap addressed: Feature selection methods are studied for general classification but not optimized for financial time series (Gap 10) | Feasibility: Technical indicators can be computed from public OHLCV data; fundamental ratios are available from SEC EDGAR and public financial APIs; macroeconomic variables are published by FRED; systematic feature selection (e.g., ablation studies, Boruta, permutation importance) is purely computational.
