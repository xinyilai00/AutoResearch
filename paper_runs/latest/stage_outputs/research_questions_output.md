CANDIDATE RESEARCH QUESTIONS:

1. Under what market conditions does NLP-based sentiment analysis provide statistically significant incremental predictive power for US equity returns beyond what is achievable using pure time-series deep learning models on OHLCV data alone? | Gap addressed: No rigorous controlled comparison exists between sentiment-driven and pure time-series prediction pipelines to determine when textual information adds value (Gap 3). | Feasibility: Public financial news corpora (e.g., Reuters, Financial Times RSS archives), Yahoo Finance OHLCV data, and pre-trained financial sentiment models (FinBERT) are freely available; incremental predictive power can be measured via Diebold-Mariano tests on out-of-sample forecasts across partitioned market conditions.

2. How does the relative ranking of leading AI stock prediction architectures (LSTM, Transformer, tree-based ensembles) change when evaluation shifts from statistical accuracy metrics (RMSE, directional accuracy) to risk-adjusted net returns after incorporating realistic transaction costs, slippage, and market impact estimates? | Gap addressed: The literature reports predictive accuracy using statistical metrics rather than risk-adjusted trading profitability, leaving a gap between model performance and real-world economic value (Gap 2). | Feasibility: Historical OHLCV data from Yahoo Finance or WRDS, published transaction cost estimates by market cap tier, and standard slippage models (e.g., square-root market impact model) are publicly documented; backtesting frameworks such as Backtrader or Zipline enable systematic cost-adjusted evaluation.

3. How do LSTM, Transformer, and gradient-boosted tree models for stock prediction perform across distinct market regimes (bull, bear, high-volatility, low-volatility), and does a regime-conditional ensemble that switches models based on detected regimes outperform any single static model? | Gap addressed: The interaction between AI-driven strategies and different market regimes is underexplored, with most studies evaluating models over fixed historical windows without regime-conditional analysis (Gap 5). | Feasibility: Market regimes can be identified using publicly available VIX data and NBER recession dates or Hidden Markov Models fitted on public index returns; regime-conditional performance can be backtested on decades of US equity data from CRSP or Yahoo Finance.

4. Can SHAP-based and attention-weight explainability frameworks reveal whether deep learning stock prediction models rely on economically interpretable features consistent with known asset pricing factors, or on spurious statistical artifacts? | Gap addressed: Explainability in AI-driven stock prediction remains almost entirely unaddressed despite growing regulatory scrutiny of black-box trading algorithms (Gap 4), and there is a disconnect between AI prediction signals and asset pricing theory (Gap 7). | Feasibility: SHAP and integrated gradients are open-source libraries applicable to any trained model; Fama-French factor data is publicly available from Kenneth French's data library; feature attribution maps can be systematically compared against known risk factors using correlation and regression analysis.

5. To what extent do deep learning stock prediction models trained on US S&P 500 equities generalize to stocks in emerging markets (e.g., India, Brazil, China) without retraining, and which architectural or training modifications most effectively enable cross-market transfer? | Gap addressed: Most AI stock prediction studies evaluate on a narrow set of markets with virtually no systematic cross-market comparative analysis (Gap 1). | Feasibility: Historical equity data for major emerging market indices is freely available via Yahoo Finance and national exchange APIs; transfer learning experiments (frozen backbone, fine-tuning, domain adaptation) can be conducted entirely computationally with no proprietary data requirements.

6. Does incorporating publicly available ESG scores and satellite-derived nighttime light intensity data as auxiliary inputs to deep learning stock prediction models improve out-of-sample return forecasts for US equities beyond traditional OHLCV and macroeconomic features? | Gap addressed: The vast majority of studies rely exclusively on traditional OHLCV data with minimal investigation of alternative data sources such as ESG metrics or satellite imagery (Gap 6). | Feasibility: ESG ratings are publicly available from MSCI (via open datasets) and Sustainalytics summaries; NOAA provides free satellite nighttime light data at monthly resolution; these can be aligned with stock return data and evaluated through controlled ablation experiments.

7. Do the predictive signals discovered by deep learning models for US equity returns survive the multiple-testing corrections advocated by Harvey et al. (2015), and can these signals be decomposed into exposure to established Fama-French risk factors versus residual alpha attributable to behavioral anomalies? | Gap addressed: Few studies evaluate whether AI-discovered signals represent genuine risk factors or data-mining artifacts, and multiple-testing corrections are rarely applied in the AI stock prediction literature (Gap 7). | Feasibility: Fama-French 5-factor and momentum factor data are publicly available; Harvey et al.'s hurdle t-statistic and Bonferroni/Holm corrections are straightforward to implement; AI-generated signals can be regressed on public factor returns to decompose sources of predictive power.

8. How does a unified multi-asset AI prediction framework covering equities, government bonds, commodities, and cryptocurrencies compare in portfolio-level risk-adjusted performance to single-asset-class AI strategies and traditional mean-variance optimization benchmarks? | Gap addressed: Multi-asset and cross-asset AI prediction remains underexplored, with most studies focusing exclusively on equities (Gap 8). | Feasibility: Historical price data for all four asset classes is freely available (Yahoo Finance for stocks and ETFs, FRED for bond yields, Quandl for commodities, CoinGecko for cryptocurrencies); portfolio optimization can be performed computationally using standard convex optimization libraries.

9. Does temporal performance degradation of deep learning stock prediction models differ across architectures (LSTM vs. Transformer vs. XGBoost), and what retraining frequency and window strategy minimizes out-of-sample decay while controlling for computational cost? | Gap addressed: Most studies evaluate models over fixed historical windows without analyzing temporal degradation or adaptive retraining strategies (Gap 5). | Feasibility: Rolling-window and expanding-window backtesting experiments can be conducted on decades of public US equity data; computational cost can be measured directly in GPU-hours; degradation curves can be compared across architectures using publicly reproducible training pipelines.

10. Can graph neural networks that model inter-stock relationships through supply chain linkages (derived from public SEC filing disclosures) improve cross-sectional return predictions compared to models that treat each stock independently? | Gap addressed: Most studies rely on traditional OHLCV data and standard indicators with minimal investigation of relational or network-based alternative data structures (Gap 6), and cross-market structural information remains underutilized (Gap 1). | Feasibility: SEC EDGAR provides free access to supply chain and major customer disclosures (Item 1A risk factors, major customer concentration filings); graph structures can be constructed from these public filings; graph neural network implementations (PyTorch Geometric) are open source and can be evaluated on public equity return data.
