CANDIDATE RESEARCH QUESTIONS:

1. Does combining clinical imaging, structured EHR variables, and clinical notes in a multimodal deep learning model yield statistically significant improvements in in-hospital mortality prediction compared to optimized unimodal baselines, when evaluated on the MIMIC-IV dataset using a standardized experimental protocol? | Gap addressed: Lack of rigorous empirical comparisons of multimodal versus unimodal AI models across clinical tasks (Gap 8) | Feasibility: MIMIC-IV is a publicly available critical care database containing linked imaging, structured data, and clinical notes for the same patient encounters, enabling controlled multimodal experiments without proprietary data.

2. To what extent do machine learning models for sepsis prediction trained on the MIMIC-IV critical care dataset degrade in performance when evaluated without retraining on the eICU Collaborative Research Database, and which domain adaptation techniques most effectively mitigate this degradation? | Gap addressed: Severe underexploration of AI model generalizability across diverse patient populations and healthcare systems (Gap 4) | Feasibility: Both MIMIC-IV and eICU are publicly accessible critical care databases from different hospital networks with different demographic and geographic profiles, enabling rigorous cross-dataset transfer experiments.

3. How does federated learning with differential privacy mechanisms affect the predictive accuracy of clinical risk models compared to centralized training, when simulated across multiple partitions of the MIMIC-III dataset designed to mimic heterogeneous institutional data distributions? | Gap addressed: Complete absence of privacy-preserving machine learning research in the healthcare AI literature despite growing regulatory requirements (Gap 7) | Feasibility: Federated learning can be simulated computationally by partitioning a single public dataset into non-IID splits that emulate multi-institutional heterogeneity, requiring no access to private hospital data.

4. Do large language models used to generate clinical discharge summaries from structured MIMIC-IV patient data produce documentation that matches human-written summaries in factual accuracy, completeness, and clinically relevant omission rates, as measured by automated and expert-proxy evaluation metrics? | Gap addressed: Absence of controlled studies examining the actual impact of LLM-assisted clinical documentation on quality and error rates (Gap 9) | Feasibility: MIMIC-IV contains both structured clinical data and human-written discharge summaries, enabling generation and comparison of LLM-produced summaries against real clinician-authored references using public data.

5. Can a simulation-based health economic model, parameterized with publicly available cost data from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project and published diagnostic accuracy figures, determine the threshold conditions under which AI-assisted radiology triage becomes cost-effective relative to standard radiologist-only workflows? | Gap addressed: Near-total absence of rigorous health economic evaluations of AI deployment in clinical workflows (Gap 1) | Feasibility: HCUP provides public hospitalization cost data, and published studies report AI diagnostic performance metrics; these can be combined in a Markov decision model or Monte Carlo simulation without requiring proprietary financial data.

6. Can a unified evaluation framework for clinical AI models, comprising standardized metrics, stratified performance reporting templates, and reproducible benchmarking protocols, be computationally validated by applying it to re-evaluate published models across multiple public clinical datasets and demonstrating improved cross-study comparability? | Gap addressed: Lack of standardized evaluation frameworks and reporting guidelines specific to healthcare AI applications (Gap 10) | Feasibility: Multiple public clinical datasets (MIMIC-IV, eICU, PhysioNet challenges) and open-source model implementations are available, enabling systematic re-evaluation and framework validation through computational experiments alone.

7. How effectively can transformer-based models applied to clinical text in the MIMIC-IV database identify and stratify mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation, and how does their performance compare across demographic subgroups to assess potential algorithmic bias? | Gap addressed: Significant underrepresentation of mental health as a clinical domain in AI research relative to radiology and critical care (Gap 6) | Feasibility: MIMIC-IV contains extensive clinical notes, psychiatric diagnoses, and medication records for a large patient cohort, enabling NLP-based mental health detection and fairness analysis using publicly available data.

8. To what degree can synthetic patient data generated by generative adversarial networks or diffusion models augment minority classes in imbalanced clinical prediction tasks, and does this approach outperform established resampling techniques such as SMOTE when evaluated on the MIMIC-III sepsis and rare disease prediction benchmarks? | Gap addressed: Persistent challenge of class imbalance in clinical datasets that limits model performance on rare but critical conditions (Gap 3, connected to Johnson & Khoshgoftaar, 2019) | Feasibility: MIMIC-III exhibits well-documented class imbalance for conditions like sepsis and rare diagnoses; synthetic data generation and comparison experiments are entirely computational and reproducible with public data.
