SUMMARY OF EXISTING WORK:

The retrieved literature spans a broad landscape of music-cognition research, with several clusters of investigation emerging. A substantial body of work examines the general cognitive benefits of musical training and engagement, including enhanced executive functioning in musicians compared to non-musicians (Zuk et al., 2014), improved cognitive control in auditory working memory (Pallesen et al., 2010), and the complex sensorimotor integration involved in instrumental practice (Seinfeld et al., 2013). Parallel to this, a growing literature investigates music-based therapeutic interventions for clinical populations, demonstrating that music listening can enhance cognitive recovery after stroke (Särkämö et al., 2008), improve verbal memory encoding through reduced prefrontal cortex activity (Ferreri et al., 2013), and offer modest cognitive benefits for individuals with dementia and Alzheimer's disease (van der Steen et al., 2018; Bleibel et al., 2023). The neurobiological underpinnings of music processing have also been explored through neuroimaging, revealing that music engages widespread bilateral networks related to attention, semantic processing, memory, and emotional regulation (Binder et al., 2009; Brattico et al., 2013).

A second major theme concerns the effects of background music on cognitive performance and learning, though findings are notably inconsistent. Some studies report that background music—particularly upbeat music—can improve processing speed and memory in older adults (Bottiroli et al., 2014), while others find no significant influence of background music on verbal learning (Jäncke & Sandmann, 2010). Theoretical frameworks including the Mozart effect and the arousal-mood hypothesis have been invoked to explain potential benefits, with evidence suggesting that working memory capacity may moderate the relationship between background music and learning outcomes (Lehmann & Seufert, 2017; Črnčec et al., 2006). Additionally, research on music and emotion has shown that music evoking different emotional states (sad vs. happy) differentially influences mind-wandering and default mode network activity (Taruffi et al., 2017), and that music interventions can reduce stress-related outcomes (de Witte et al., 2019). The evolutionary and social dimensions of music have also been addressed, with scholars arguing that music functions as a coevolved system for social bonding (Savage et al., 2020) and contributes to psychological well-being across multiple dimensions (Croom, 2014).

However, the specific intersection of classical music and attention span remains conspicuously underexplored. While the literature addresses music and cognition broadly, and background music effects on various cognitive tasks, no retrieved study isolates classical music as the independent variable with sustained attention or attention span as the primary dependent measure. The existing work tends to treat "music" as a monolithic category or uses generic musical stimuli without differentiating by genre, structural complexity, or historical period. This leaves a significant gap in understanding whether the particular structural properties of classical music—its harmonic complexity, dynamic range, temporal organization, and lack of lyrics—confer specific advantages or disadvantages for attentional processes compared to other musical genres or silence.

GAPS:
1. No study in the retrieved literature directly investigates the effect of listening to classical music (as a specific genre) on sustained attention span as a primary outcome measure, leaving the core research question entirely unaddressed.
2. The literature does not differentiate between sub-genres or periods within classical music (e.g., Baroque, Classical, Romantic, contemporary classical) and their potentially differential effects on attentional processes, despite these sub-genres varying substantially in tempo, harmonic complexity, and structural predictability.
3. There is a clear contradiction in the existing literature regarding background music's effects on cognition: some studies find beneficial effects on processing speed and memory (Bottiroli et al., 2014), while others find null effects on verbal learning (Jäncke & Sandmann, 2010), and this contradiction has not been resolved specifically for classical music or for attention-related outcomes.
4. No study examines the neurophysiological mechanisms by which classical music might specifically modulate attention networks (e.g., the dorsal and ventral attention networks, alerting, orienting, and executive control subsystems), despite neuroimaging evidence that music engages attention-related brain regions.
5. The dose-response relationship between classical music exposure and attention span—encompassing duration of listening sessions, frequency of exposure, volume levels, and cumulative effects over time—has not been systematically investigated.
6. Individual difference variables such as musical training history, personality traits, baseline attentional capacity, age, and cultural background have not been examined as potential moderators of classical music's effect on attention span, despite evidence that these factors moderate other music-cognition relationships (Lehmann & Seufert, 2017; Jacoby et al., 2020).
7. No study has investigated the effects of classical music on attention span in populations with known attentional deficits, such as individuals with ADHD, despite the clinical relevance and the existing use of music-based interventions in other cognitive domains.
8. The role of lyrical content versus purely instrumental music in affecting attention has not been systematically isolated; classical music is predominantly instrumental, but no study has directly compared classical instrumental music against lyrical music of matched complexity to determine whether the absence of lyrics is the operative factor for attention.
9. Modern, validated behavioral measures of sustained attention (e.g., the Sustained Attention to Response Task, continuous performance tasks, or ecological momentary assessment of attention in real-world settings) have not been employed in conjunction with classical music listening paradigms.
10. The temporal dynamics of attention during classical music listening—whether attention fluctuates with musical structure (e.g., tension and resolution, thematic development, dynamic contrasts)—remain unexplored, despite the existence of predictive coding frameworks for music perception (Vuust & Witek, 2014) that could inform such investigation.
11. Cross-cultural perspectives on classical music and attention are entirely absent; the retrieved literature calls for greater cross-cultural work in music cognition (Jacoby et al., 2020), but no study has examined whether Western classical music affects attention differently in listeners from different cultural backgrounds or musical enculturation histories.
12. Longitudinal studies examining whether regular, sustained exposure to classical music produces lasting changes in attentional capacity or trait-level attention are not present in the literature, leaving open the question of whether any acute effects translate into enduring cognitive benefits.
