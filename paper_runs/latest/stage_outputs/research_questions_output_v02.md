CANDIDATE RESEARCH QUESTIONS:
1. What is the cross-sectional association between daily coffee consumption frequency and self-reported depression and anxiety symptom severity among US adolescents aged 12-19, after adjusting for sleep duration, physical activity, screen time, and socioeconomic status? | Gap addressed: Gap 1 — no retrieved study directly investigates coffee consumption and mental health outcomes specifically in adolescent populations. | Feasibility: The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) provides publicly available, nationally representative data on dietary intake (including coffee), validated mental health screening instruments (PHQ-9, GAD-7 in recent waves), sleep duration, physical activity, and demographic covariates for adolescents aged 12-19 across multiple survey cycles, enabling robust multivariable regression analysis.

2. Does sleep duration and sleep quality mediate the relationship between coffee consumption and depressive symptoms in adolescents, and what proportion of the total effect operates through the sleep pathway versus direct pharmacological pathways? | Gap addressed: Gap 3 — the mediating pathway from coffee consumption to adolescent mental health via sleep disruption is implied but has not been formally tested as a causal chain. | Feasibility: NHANES and the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study both contain self-reported or actigraphy-measured sleep data, caffeine/beverage intake, and validated mental health scales for large adolescent samples, enabling formal mediation analysis using structural equation modeling or causal mediation decomposition methods with publicly available data.

3. Does the dose-response relationship between coffee intake and anxiety symptom dimensions in adolescents exhibit a threshold effect, and if so, at what daily consumption level do adverse mental health effects become statistically detectable? | Gap addressed: Gap 9 — the dose-response relationship between coffee intake and specific mental health symptom dimensions in adolescents has not been characterized, and Gap 4 — no adolescent-specific dosage thresholds exist. | Feasibility: NHANES dietary recall data captures graded coffee consumption frequency and quantity, which can be linked to anxiety subscale scores; restricted cubic spline regression and segmented regression models can identify nonlinear thresholds using these publicly available data without requiring any new data collection.

4. Does biological sex moderate the association between coffee consumption and adolescent mental health outcomes, and do observed differences align with known sex-specific variation in CYP1A2-mediated caffeine metabolism rates? | Gap addressed: Gap 8 — potential sex differences in the coffee-mental health relationship remain unexplored despite known sex differences in caffeine metabolism and adolescent depression/anxiety prevalence. | Feasibility: NHANES provides sex-disaggregated data on coffee intake, mental health outcomes, and pubertal age indicators for thousands of adolescents; multi-group structural equation modeling and interaction-term regression can test moderation, while published CYP1A2 pharmacokinetic parameters from public databases (e.g., PharmGKB) enable computational integration of metabolic sex differences into the analysis.

5. Does coffee consumption moderate the established associations between screen time, physical inactivity, and academic stress with adolescent depressive and anxiety symptoms, and to what extent does adjusting for coffee intake alter effect size estimates for these known risk factors? | Gap addressed: Gap 10 — no study examines coffee consumption as a confounding or moderating variable in established adolescent mental health risk factors. | Feasibility: NHANES and the Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System (YRBSS) contain concurrent measures of screen time, physical activity, coffee/caffeine intake, and mental health indicators for large adolescent samples, enabling moderation and confounding analyses through stratified regression and interaction models using entirely public data.

6. What longitudinal trajectories of coffee consumption during adolescence (ages 12-18) predict internalizing disorder symptom severity in young adulthood (ages 19-26), and does timing of initiation (early vs. late adolescence) differentially predict outcomes? | Gap addressed: Gap 7 — longitudinal data on adolescent coffee consumption patterns and subsequent mental health trajectories into young adulthood are entirely absent. | Feasibility: The National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health) follows a nationally representative cohort from adolescence into adulthood with repeated measures of dietary behaviors including coffee intake and validated mental health assessments across five waves, enabling growth curve modeling and longitudinal panel analysis with publicly accessible restricted-use or public-use data files.

7. Is the association between coffee consumption and adolescent mental health outcomes better explained by total caffeine exposure or by non-caffeine bioactive compounds in coffee, as inferred by comparing the mental health profiles of adolescent coffee drinkers versus consumers of other caffeinated beverages (energy drinks, soda, tea)? | Gap addressed: Gap 5 — no study distinguishes the mental health effects of coffee as a whole beverage from those of isolated caffeine in adolescent populations. | Feasibility: NHANES dietary recall data differentiates between coffee, tea, energy drinks, and caffeinated soft drinks, allowing comparative analysis of mental health outcomes across beverage types while controlling for estimated total caffeine intake; this natural experiment design leverages existing variation in beverage choice among adolescents in publicly available survey data.

8. Does evening chronotype in adolescents predict higher coffee consumption as a compensatory behavior, and does this coffee-chronotype interaction produce a synergistic negative effect on sleep quality and mental health outcomes beyond the additive effects of each factor alone? | Gap addressed: Gap 6 — the interaction between coffee consumption and chronotype in adolescents has not been investigated. | Feasibility: The ABCD Study and several publicly available adolescent cohort datasets (e.g., the School Sleep Habits Survey archived in the Sleep and Behavior Research Repository) include Morningness-Eveningness Scale for Children scores, caffeine/beverage intake diaries, sleep diaries or actigraphy, and mental health questionnaires, enabling interaction-effect modeling and latent profile analysis with existing public data.
