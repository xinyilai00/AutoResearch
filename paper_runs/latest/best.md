# Can Epistemic Uncertainty Estimates Reliably Discriminate Between Correct and Incorrect AI-Generated Scientific Conclusions? A Multi-Domain Framework and Evaluation Protocol

## Abstract

As artificial intelligence systems are increasingly deployed to assist in scientific reasoningâfrom hypothesis generation to data interpretationâa fundamental question arises: can these systems reliably detect when their own scientific conclusions are wrong? This paper investigates whether epistemic uncertainty estimates, derived from Bayesian neural networks and deep ensembles, can serve as a discriminative signal for identifying substantively incorrect scientific conclusions generated by AI models. We propose a multi-domain evaluation framework in which AI systems are tasked with producing scientific conclusions from structured data, after which synthetically injected errors of varying severity and type (statistical misinterpretations, causal violations, methodological flaws, and interpretive overreach) are introduced into the reasoning pipeline. The core hypothesis is that epistemic uncertaintyâreflecting model ignorance rather than data noiseâwill be systematically elevated for incorrect conclusions, thereby providing a usable self-monitoring signal. We review the relevant literature spanning uncertainty quantification, hallucination detection in large language models, causal reasoning, and metacognitive architectures, identifying ten critical gaps that motivate this investigation. Because the deep literature review, experimental execution, and verified citation stages remain pending at the time of this writing, this paper presents a comprehensive research framework, detailed methodology, and provisional analytical structure rather than completed empirical results. We articulate the experimental design, specify success criteria, describe the synthetic error injection protocol, and outline the cross-domain evaluation strategy across physics, biomedicine, and social science. The framework is intended to be directly executable by subsequent research stages and to serve as a reference architecture for the emerging field of AI scientific self-evaluation.

## Introduction

The accelerating integration of artificial intelligence into scientific research workflows has transformed how hypotheses are generated, experiments are designed, and conclusions are drawn from complex datasets. Large language models now assist researchers in literature synthesis, while deep learning architectures process high-dimensional experimental data in domains ranging from protein structure prediction to climate modeling. Yet this growing reliance on AI-generated scientific reasoning raises a profound epistemological challenge: when an AI system produces a scientific conclusion, can it also assess whether that conclusion is likely to be wrong?

This question is not merely academic. The consequences of undetected errors in AI-generated scientific claims are substantial. In biomedical research, an incorrect conclusion about drug efficacy or disease mechanism could propagate through the literature if researchers trust AI outputs without sufficient scrutiny. In materials science, a flawed prediction about material properties could lead to costly experimental dead ends. In policy-relevant domains such as climate science or epidemiology, erroneous AI-generated conclusions could inform decisions with wide-ranging societal impacts. The problem is compounded by the fact that AI-generated scientific text is often fluent, internally coherent, and superficially plausibleâmaking errors difficult to detect through casual inspection.

The distinction between different types of uncertainty provides a conceptual foundation for addressing this challenge. HÃ¼llermeier and Waegeman (2021) established a taxonomy that separates **aleatoric uncertainty**âarising from inherent noise or stochasticity in the dataâfrom **epistemic uncertainty**âarising from the model's limited knowledge or insufficient training data. Aleatoric uncertainty is irreducible: no amount of additional model capacity can eliminate the noise inherent in a measurement process. Epistemic uncertainty, by contrast, is in principle reducible: it reflects gaps in the model's understanding that could be closed with more data, better architecture, or improved training procedures. This distinction is crucial for the present investigation because it is epistemic uncertaintyâuncertainty about whether the model has correctly learned the relevant scientific relationshipsâthat is most likely to signal when a generated conclusion is substantively wrong.

Consider a concrete scenario. An AI system is given experimental data from a clinical trial and asked to conclude whether a treatment is effective. If the training data included many similar trials and the model has learned the relevant statistical and causal relationships, epistemic uncertainty should be low, and the conclusion is likely reliable. If, however, the trial involves a novel drug mechanism or an unusual patient population that falls outside the model's training distribution, epistemic uncertainty should be elevatedâsignaling that the model is operating in a regime where its conclusions are less trustworthy. The central question of this paper is whether this theoretical expectation holds in practice, and whether epistemic uncertainty estimates can be calibrated to serve as a practical self-monitoring mechanism for AI-generated scientific conclusions.

The challenge is multifaceted. First, scientific conclusions are not simple classification labels; they are complex, multi-sentence claims that integrate statistical evidence, causal reasoning, methodological assessment, and domain-specific interpretation. Second, errors in scientific conclusions are not binaryâthey range from subtle statistical misinterpretations to gross causal violations, and the detectability of an error may depend on its type and severity. Third, the relationship between epistemic uncertainty and conclusion correctness may vary across scientific domains with different epistemic structures: physics, with its well-defined theoretical frameworks, may exhibit different uncertainty-error relationships than biology, with its complex emergent systems, or social science, with its context-dependent findings.

This paper addresses these challenges through a structured research framework. We propose a multi-domain evaluation protocol in which AI models generate scientific conclusions from standardized datasets, synthetically injected errors of categorized types and severities are introduced into the reasoning pipeline, and epistemic uncertainty estimates are evaluated for their ability to discriminate between correct and incorrect conclusions. The framework is designed to be directly executable and to produce measurable, comparable results across domains and error types.

## Review

### Uncertainty Quantification in Machine Learning

The formal treatment of uncertainty in machine learning has a long history, but it has gained renewed urgency with the deployment of deep learning systems in high-stakes domains. HÃ¼llermeier and Waegeman (2021) provided what has become the standard taxonomy, distinguishing aleatoric uncertainty (data-inherent noise) from epistemic uncertainty (model-related ignorance). This distinction, originally articulated in Bayesian statistics and decision theory, was operationalized for deep learning by Gal and Ghahramani (2016), who showed that dropout at test time approximates Bayesian inference in deep Gaussian processes, yielding tractable epistemic uncertainty estimates from standard neural network architectures [TODO: verify full citation].

Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) offer a principled framework for uncertainty estimation by placing distributions over model parameters rather than point estimates. Jospin et al. (2022) provided a comprehensive survey of practical BNN methods, including mean-field variational inference, Monte Carlo dropout, and deep ensembles. Deep ensembles, introduced by Lakshminarayanan et al. (2017), estimate epistemic uncertainty by training multiple models with different random initializations and measuring the disagreement among their predictions [TODO: verify full citation]. While less theoretically grounded than full Bayesian inference, deep ensembles have proven empirically effective and computationally tractable, making them a popular choice for uncertainty-aware applications.

The calibration of uncertainty estimatesâthe degree to which predicted confidence matches empirical accuracyâhas been extensively studied for classification tasks. Guo et al. (2017) demonstrated that modern neural networks are often poorly calibrated, exhibiting overconfidence in their predictions [TODO: verify full citation]. Temperature scaling and other post-hoc calibration methods can improve calibration on held-out data, but their effectiveness for novel, out-of-distribution inputs remains contested. Critically, **calibration has not been systematically studied in the context of AI-generated scientific conclusions**, where the "predictions" are complex textual claims rather than class labels, and where ground truth may itself be uncertain or contested.

A significant limitation of existing uncertainty quantification methods is that they are designed for standard prediction tasksâclassification, regression, sequence labelingârather than for the meta-level question of whether a generated scientific conclusion is substantively correct. A model may produce well-calibrated uncertainty estimates for individual data points while remaining systematically wrong about the scientific interpretation of those data points. For example, a model might correctly predict that a drug reduces blood pressure (low uncertainty) while incorrectly concluding that the mechanism is vasodilation rather than diuresis (a scientific error that standard uncertainty estimates would not capture). This gap motivates the present investigation: can uncertainty estimation methods be adapted or extended to operate at the level of scientific conclusions rather than individual predictions?

### Hallucination and Error Detection in Large Language Models

The proliferation of large language models (LLMs) has brought the problem of "hallucination"âthe generation of fluent but factually incorrect textâto the forefront of AI research. Bang et al. (2023) systematically evaluated ChatGPT's hallucination tendencies across multiple reasoning tasks, finding that the model frequently generates plausible-sounding but incorrect claims, particularly in domains requiring specialized knowledge. The MFOUR Vibe Framework (Hemachandra et al., 2023) attempted to impose determinism on LLM outputs through structured prompting and verification loops, but acknowledged that inherent stochasticity limits the reliability of such approaches.

The hallucination detection literature has developed several strategies for identifying incorrect LLM outputs. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) cross-references generated claims against external knowledge bases, flagging inconsistencies between the generated text and retrieved evidence. Self-consistency methods generate multiple outputs for the same input and flag cases where the model produces inconsistent answers. Confidence-based methods use the model's own probability estimates (logit-based or verbalized) to identify low-confidence outputs that may be unreliable.

However, these methods are designed primarily for detecting **factual hallucinations**âclaims that contradict established facts or source text. They are less well-suited for detecting what we term **scientific hallucinations**: conclusions that are factually plausible and internally coherent but scientifically invalid. A scientific hallucination might involve correct individual facts assembled into an incorrect causal argument, correct statistical results subjected to inappropriate interpretation, or correct methodological descriptions applied to an unsuitable experimental design. These errors are particularly dangerous because they evade standard fact-checking: every individual claim in the conclusion may be true, yet the overall scientific argument is flawed.

DÃ­az-RodrÃ­guez et al. (2023) and Lekadir et al. (2025) proposed comprehensive frameworks for trustworthy AI that encompass robustness, transparency, ethics, and legal compliance. While these frameworks provide valuable design principles for building more reliable AI systems, they focus on preventing errors at the system level rather than on enabling runtime self-monitoring of specific outputs. The gap between system-level trustworthiness and output-level self-evaluation remains substantial, and it is this gap that the present framework aims to address.

### Causal Reasoning and Knowledge Representation

The importance of causal reasoning for scientific AI has been forcefully argued by SchÃ¶lkopf et al. (2021), who contended that causal representation learning is essential for AI systems to generalize beyond their training distributions. Causal models, unlike purely correlational ones, capture the directional dependencies between variables, enabling reasoning about interventions and counterfactuals. For the present investigation, causal reasoning is relevant because many scientific errors involve violations of causal structureâconfusing correlation with causation, ignoring confounders, or misidentifying mediating variables.

Knowledge graphs (Ji et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2014) provide structured representations of domain knowledge that could, in principle, serve as reference standards against which AI-generated conclusions are validated. If an AI system concludes that "variable A causes variable B," this claim could be checked against a knowledge graph encoding established causal relationships in the relevant domain. However, knowledge graphs are inherently incomplete, particularly in rapidly evolving scientific fields, and they may not capture the nuanced, context-dependent nature of many scientific claims. Moreover, **no integrated system currently uses causal knowledge bases to automatically flag AI-generated scientific conclusions that violate known causal constraints** (Gap 6 from the literature review).

Clark's (2013) predictive processing framework and Stephan et al.'s (2016) metacognitive Bayesian model offer cognitive architectures in which self-monitoring emerges naturally from prediction-error minimization. In these frameworks, the brain continuously generates predictions about incoming sensory data and uses the discrepancy between predictions and observations (prediction error) to update its internal models. Metacognitionâthinking about thinkingâarises when the system applies this same predictive machinery to its own internal states. While these ideas have inspired AI architectures for self-monitoring, they have not been operationalized in systems tasked with evaluating the scientific validity of their own outputs.

### Self-Correction and Metacognition in AI Agents

The autonomous agents survey by Wang et al. (2024) describes LLM-based agents equipped with planning, reflection, and self-correction capabilities. These agents can identify and correct errors in their own outputs, but the errors they detect are typically **procedural or syntactic**âincorrect code, malformed queries, logical inconsistencies in step-by-step reasoningârather than **substantive scientific errors**. An agent might detect that it has made an arithmetic error in calculating a p-value, but it is far less clear whether it can detect that the statistical test itself was inappropriate for the data structure, or that the causal interpretation of a significant result is unwarranted.

Multi-agent architectures, in which multiple AI instances independently address the same problem and then compare or debate their conclusions, offer a potential mechanism for error detection through disagreement. If two agents independently derive different scientific conclusions from the same data, the disagreement itself signals that at least one conclusion may be wrong. However, this approach faces the challenge of correlated errors: if all agents share the same training data and architectural biases, they may make the same mistakes, producing false consensus rather than productive disagreement.

The relationship between these agent-based approaches and uncertainty quantification remains underexplored. In principle, the disagreement among multiple agents could serve as an ensemble-based epistemic uncertainty estimate, analogous to the prediction variance in deep ensembles. Whether this analogy holds for complex scientific reasoning tasksâwhere outputs are not scalar predictions but structured argumentsâis an open empirical question that the present framework is designed to address.

### Summary of Gaps and Motivation

The literature review reveals a landscape in which the individual components necessary for AI scientific self-evaluationâuncertainty quantification, hallucination detection, causal reasoning, knowledge representation, and metacognitive architecturesâall exist, but have not been integrated into a coherent framework for detecting errors in AI-generated scientific conclusions. The ten gaps identified in the upstream literature review (summarized in the Introduction and detailed in the stage inputs) collectively define the problem space. The selected research questionâwhether epistemic uncertainty estimates can discriminate between correct and incorrect AI-generated scientific conclusionsâaddresses Gap 2 directly while providing a methodological foundation for addressing Gaps 1, 3, 7, 8, 9, and 10.

## Methodology

### Overview and Design Philosophy

The proposed methodology is designed as a controlled experimental framework that can be executed across multiple scientific domains. The core design principle is **synthetic error injection**: rather than waiting for AI systems to naturally produce errors (which would be difficult to control and categorize), we systematically introduce errors of known type and severity into the AI's reasoning pipeline and then measure whether epistemic uncertainty estimates can detect these injected errors. This approach provides ground truth for evaluation while allowing precise control over error characteristics.

The methodology comprises five stages: (1) domain and dataset selection, (2) baseline conclusion generation, (3) synthetic error injection, (4) uncertainty estimation, and (5) discriminative evaluation. Each stage is detailed below.

Three scientific domains are selected to span a range of epistemic structures:

**Physics (condensed matter / materials science).** Physics is characterized by well-defined theoretical frameworks, precise mathematical relationships, and relatively low-dimensional data. **Social science (economics / political science).** Social science is characterized by context-dependent findings, complex causal structures with numerous confounders, and contested theoretical frameworks. Scientific conclusions in social science often involve causal claims about human behavior that are difficult to validate against definitive ground truth. The selection of these three domains is motivated by their differing epistemic structures, which allow us to test whether the relationship between epistemic uncertainty and conclusion correctness generalizes across fields with different characteristics. Physics represents a domain with strong theoretical constraints and relatively clear ground truth; biomedicine represents a domain with moderate theoretical constraints and noisy ground truth; social science represents a domain with weak theoretical constraints and contested ground truth.

### Stage 2: Baseline Conclusion Generation

For each domain, AI models are tasked with generating scientific conclusions from standardized input data. The models include:

- **Bayesian neural networks** (implemented via mean-field variational inference or MC dropout) for structured prediction tasks within each domain.
- **Deep ensembles** (5â10 independently trained models with different random initializations) for the same prediction tasks.
- **Large language models** (GPT-4, Claude, or open-source alternatives) prompted to generate scientific conclusions from the same input data, with epistemic uncertainty estimated via verbalized confidence, logit-based probabilities, or multiple sampling.

The baseline conclusions are generated under standard conditions, without injected errors, to establish the models' typical performance and uncertainty profiles. Each conclusion is evaluated by domain experts (or against established ground truth where available) to confirm correctness.

### Stage 3: Synthetic Error Injection

This is the core experimental manipulation. Four categories of errors are systematically injected into the AI's reasoning pipeline:

**Type 1: Statistical misinterpretations.** These errors involve incorrect application or interpretation of statistical methods. Examples include: treating a non-significant result as evidence of no effect (absence of evidence fallacy); interpreting correlation as causation without appropriate causal identification; applying parametric tests to non-normal data without transformation; reporting p-values incorrectly; or committing the base rate fallacy in probabilistic reasoning. Severity levels range from subtle (e.g., a minor degrees-of-freedom error that slightly inflates significance) to gross (e.g., completely misidentifying the statistical test used).

**Type 2: Causal violations.** These errors involve incorrect causal reasoning. Examples include: reversing the direction of causation; ignoring a critical confounder; claiming mediation without testing the mediating pathway; committing the ecological fallacy (inferring individual-level relationships from aggregate data); or positing causal mechanisms that violate known physical or biological constraints. Severity levels range from subtle (e.g., omitting a weak confounder) to gross (e.g., claiming that a downstream effect causes its upstream cause).

**Type 3: Methodological flaws.** These errors involve inappropriate experimental or analytical methods. Examples include: using an inadequate sample size for the claimed effect size; applying a measurement instrument outside its validated range; failing to account for multiple comparisons; using an inappropriate control group; or selecting a time window that introduces survivorship bias. Severity levels range from subtle (e.g., a borderline sample size) to gross (e.g., no control group in a causal claim).

**Type 4: Interpretive overreach.** These errors involve conclusions that extend beyond what the data support. Examples include: generalizing from a specific population to all populations without justification; claiming a mechanism when only an association was demonstrated; extrapolating beyond the range of observed data; or presenting a speculative hypothesis as a confirmed finding. Severity levels range from subtle (e.g., slightly overstating the generality of a finding) to gross (e.g., claiming a breakthrough treatment based on in vitro results alone).

Each error type is injected at three severity levels (subtle, moderate, gross), yielding a 4 Ã 3 factorial design with 12 error conditions per domain. For each condition, a minimum of 50 error-injected conclusions are generated (total: 4 Ã 3 Ã 50 = 600 error-injected conclusions per domain, 1,800 across all three domains). An equal number of correct conclusions serve as controls.

The error injection is implemented through two mechanisms: (a) **prompt-level injection**, where the LLM is given instructions that lead it to commit specific errors (e.g., "ignore the potential confounding effect of variable X"), and (b) **data-level injection**, where the input data are modified to create conditions that naturally elicit errors (e.g., removing a confounder from the dataset so that the model cannot account for it). Both mechanisms are used to ensure that the results are not artifacts of a single injection method.

### Stage 4: Uncertainty Estimation

For each generated conclusion (both correct and error-injected), epistemic uncertainty is estimated using the following methods:

**For Bayesian neural networks and deep ensembles:**
- **Predictive variance decomposition:** The total predictive variance is decomposed into aleatoric and epistemic components using the standard variance decomposition formula: Var(Y) = E[Var(Y|Î¸)] + Var(E[Y|Î¸]), where the first term is aleatoric uncertainty and the second is epistemic uncertainty.
- **Mutual information:** The mutual information between predictions and model parameters is computed as an alternative epistemic uncertainty measure.
- **Ensemble disagreement:** For deep ensembles, the standard deviation or entropy of predictions across ensemble members serves as an epistemic uncertainty proxy.

**For large language models:**
- **Verbalized confidence:** The model is prompted to assign a confidence score (0â100%) to each conclusion it generates.
- **Logit-based probability:** The token-level probabilities for key claims in the conclusion are extracted and aggregated (e.g., minimum probability, mean probability, or probability of the most uncertain token).
- **Multiple sampling:** The model generates N (e.g., 10) independent conclusions for the same input, and the semantic similarity or agreement among these conclusions serves as an uncertainty proxy.
- **Self-reflection confidence:** The model is prompted to critique its own conclusion and assign a revised confidence score; the difference between initial and revised confidence serves as an uncertainty signal.

### Stage 5: Discriminative Evaluation

The core evaluation question is whether epistemic uncertainty estimates can discriminate between correct and incorrect conclusions. This is assessed using the following metrics:

**Discrimination metrics:**
- **Area under the ROC curve (AUROC):** Measures the overall ability of the uncertainty estimate to rank incorrect conclusions higher than correct ones.
- **Area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC):** Measures discrimination performance with emphasis on the positive class (incorrect conclusions), which is important when the base rate of errors is low.
- **Brier score:** Measures the accuracy of probabilistic predictions (whether the uncertainty-calibrated probability of correctness matches the empirical outcome).

**Calibration metrics:**
- **Expected calibration error (ECE):** Measures the average discrepancy between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy across confidence bins.
- **Maximum calibration error (MCE):** Measures the worst-case discrepancy across confidence bins.
- **Reliability diagrams:** Visualize the relationship between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy.

**Stratified analysis:**
All metrics are computed stratified by:
- Domain (physics, biomedicine, social science)
- Error type (statistical, causal, methodological, interpretive)
- Error severity (subtle, moderate, gross)
- Uncertainty estimation method (BNN, deep ensemble, verbalized confidence, logit-based, multiple sampling, self-reflection)

This stratification allows us to identify not only whether uncertainty estimation works overall, but also **where it works and where it fails**âwhich domains, error types, and severity levels are amenable to uncertainty-based error detection, and which are systematically undetectable.

### Success Criteria

The framework defines the following success criteria, ordered from most to least stringent:

1. **Strong success:** AUROC â¥ 0.85 for discriminating correct from incorrect conclusions across all three domains and all four error types, with ECE â¤ 0.05.
2. **Moderate success:** AUROC â¥ 0.75 for at least two of three domains and at least three of four error types, with ECE â¤ 0.10.
3. **Partial success:** AUROC â¥ 0.70 for at least one domain and at least two error types, with identifiable patterns explaining where and why the method succeeds or fails.
4. **Informative failure:** AUROC < 0.70 across most conditions, but with clear diagnostic information about why uncertainty estimation fails for scientific error detection (e.g., systematic underconfidence for correct conclusions, or no separation between uncertainty distributions for correct and incorrect outputs).

### Experimental Controls

Several controls are implemented to ensure the validity of the results:

- **Blinded evaluation:** Domain experts evaluating conclusion correctness are blinded to whether the conclusion was generated under baseline or error-injected conditions.
- **Counterbalanced ordering:** The order in which conclusions are presented to evaluators is randomized to prevent order effects.
- **Inter-rater reliability:** Multiple experts evaluate each conclusion, and inter-rater agreement (Cohen's kappa or Krippendorff's alpha) is reported.
- **Model diversity:** Multiple model architectures and sizes are tested to ensure that results are not specific to a single model.

### Provisional Hypotheses

Based on the literature reviewed above, the following provisional hypotheses will be tested:

**H1:** Epistemic uncertainty estimates from deep ensembles will achieve higher AUROC than those from Bayesian neural networks (MC dropout), because ensemble disagreement captures a broader range of model uncertainty than the approximate posterior sampled by dropout.

**H2:** Gross errors will be more detectable than subtle errors across all uncertainty methods, with a monotonic relationship between error severity and AUROC.

**H3:** Causal violations will be less detectable than statistical misinterpretations, because causal errors involve higher-level reasoning that is less directly reflected in the model's predictive uncertainty.

**H4:** Physics conclusions will exhibit better uncertainty-error discrimination than social science conclusions, because the stronger theoretical constraints in physics create a tighter coupling between model uncertainty and conclusion correctness.

**H5:** Self-reflection confidence (the change in confidence after self-critique) will provide complementary information to logit-based probabilities, improving discrimination when combined in a simple ensemble.

**H6:** Verbalized confidence will be poorly calibrated (high ECE) relative to logit-based and ensemble-based methods, consistent with prior findings that LLMs exhibit overconfidence in verbalized assessments.

## Discussion

### Interpretation of the Framework

The methodology proposed in this paper represents, to our knowledge, the first systematic attempt to evaluate whether epistemic uncertainty estimates can serve as a self-monitoring signal for AI-generated scientific conclusions. The framework's design reflects several deliberate choices that warrant discussion.

The decision to use **synthetic error injection** rather than naturally occurring errors is a pragmatic one. Natural errors in AI-generated scientific conclusions are difficult to control, categorize, and validate. By injecting errors of known type and severity, we obtain ground truth labels that enable rigorous evaluation. However, this choice introduces a potential threat to ecological validity: injected errors may differ systematically from the errors that AI systems produce naturally. A model that can detect injected errors may still fail to detect its own spontaneous errors, which may arise from different mechanisms (e.g., training data biases, architectural limitations, or emergent behaviors). Future work should complement this synthetic approach with studies of naturally occurring errors, once sufficient corpora of AI-generated scientific text with expert-validated correctness labels become available.

The **multi-domain design** is intended to test the generalizability of uncertainty-based error detection across scientific fields with different epistemic structures. If the method works well in physics but poorly in social science, this would suggest that the approach is most applicable in domains with strong theoretical constraints, where model uncertainty is more tightly coupled to conclusion correctness. Conversely, if the method works comparably across domains, this would support the generality of epistemic uncertainty as a self-monitoring signal. Either outcome is scientifically informative.

### Anticipated Challenges

Several challenges are anticipated in the execution of this framework:

**Challenge 1: Ground truth ambiguity in social science.** Unlike physics, where theoretical predictions often provide definitive ground truth, social science findings are frequently context-dependent and contested. A conclusion that is "correct" in one theoretical framework may be "incorrect" in another. This ambiguity complicates the binary correct/incorrect labeling required for AUROC computation. We plan to address this by using multiple expert raters and reporting results both with majority-vote labels and with continuous correctness scores reflecting expert agreement.

**Challenge 2: Semantic uncertainty estimation for LLMs.** While epistemic uncertainty is well-defined for Bayesian neural networks (as posterior variance over parameters), it is less clearly defined for large language models. Verbalized confidence and logit-based probabilities are imperfect proxies for epistemic uncertainty, and they may conflate epistemic and aleatoric components. The multiple-sampling approach provides a more direct analogy to ensemble disagreement, but semantic similarity between generated conclusions is difficult to quantify reliably. We plan to evaluate multiple semantic similarity metrics (cosine similarity of embeddings, ROUGE scores, and human-rated agreement) and report sensitivity analyses.

**Challenge 3: Scalability of expert evaluation.** The framework requires expert evaluation of 3,600 conclusions (1,800 error-injected + 1,800 correct controls across three domains). This is a substantial annotation effort. We plan to mitigate this by using a combination of expert evaluation (for a stratified subset) and automated correctness verification (for the full set), with the expert subset serving to validate the automated labels.

**Challenge 4: Confounding between error injection method and detectability.** Errors injected via prompt manipulation may be more or less detectable than errors injected via data manipulation, independent of the error type itself. We address this by using both injection methods and including injection method as a factor in the analysis.

### Relationship to Broader AI Safety and Reliability

The question of whether AI can detect its own scientific errors is a specific instance of the broader challenge of AI self-awareness and self-monitoring. In the AI safety literature, this is related to the concept of **corrigibility**âthe ability of an AI system to recognize that it may be wrong and to accept correction. The present framework operationalizes a limited form of corrigibility: the ability to flag one's own outputs as potentially unreliable based on internal uncertainty signals.

The framework also relates to the growing literature on **AI epistemic humility**âthe idea that AI systems should express appropriate uncertainty about their claims, particularly in domains where errors have significant consequences. If epistemic uncertainty estimates can reliably discriminate between correct and incorrect scientific conclusions, they provide a mechanism for AI systems to communicate their limitations to human users, enabling more informed decisions about when to trust AI outputs and when to seek additional verification.

### Limitations

Several limitations of the proposed framework should be acknowledged:

1. **Synthetic errors may not reflect natural errors.** As discussed above, the ecological validity of injected errors is uncertain.

2. **The framework evaluates discrimination, not correction.** Detecting that a conclusion is wrong is necessary but not sufficient for correcting it. The framework does not evaluate whether the AI system can identify the specific error and produce a corrected conclusion.

3. **Domain selection is limited to three fields.** While physics, biomedicine, and social science span a range of epistemic structures, they do not encompass all scientific domains. Results may not generalize to fields such as mathematics, engineering, or the humanities.

4. **The framework assumes access to model internals.** For BNNs and deep ensembles, uncertainty estimation requires access to model parameters or multiple model instances. For proprietary LLMs accessed via API, only verbalized confidence and multiple-sampling methods are available, limiting the applicability of the full framework.

5. **Ground truth for scientific conclusions is inherently provisional.** Even expert-validated "correct" conclusions may later be shown to be wrong as scientific knowledge advances. The framework evaluates discrimination against current consensus, not against ultimate truth.

6. **Deep literature review, experimental execution, and verified citations are pending.** The theoretical foundations and methodological design presented here have not yet been validated through comprehensive systematic review or empirical testing. All hypotheses are provisional and subject to revision based on subsequent stages.

### Implications for Practice

If the framework yields positive results (moderate to strong success per the criteria defined in Methodology), the practical implications are substantial. AI systems equipped with reliable self-monitoring could:

- **Flag uncertain conclusions for human review**, reducing the risk that erroneous scientific claims enter the literature unchallenged.
- **Guide resource allocation** in research workflows, directing human attention to the AI outputs that most need verification.
- **Enable adaptive confidence thresholds**, where the acceptable level of uncertainty varies with the stakes of the decision (e.g., lower thresholds for exploratory hypothesis generation, higher thresholds for clinical recommendations).
- **Support regulatory compliance** in domains where AI-generated scientific claims must meet evidentiary standards (e.g., drug approval, environmental impact assessment).

If the framework yields negative results (informative failure), the implications are equally important: they would suggest that current uncertainty estimation methods are insufficient for scientific self-monitoring, motivating the development of new approaches specifically designed for this task.

## Conclusion

This paper has presented a comprehensive research framework for investigating whether epistemic uncertainty estimates from Bayesian neural networks and deep ensembles can reliably discriminate between correct and substantively incorrect scientific conclusions generated by AI models. The framework addresses a critical gap at the intersection of uncertainty quantification, hallucination detection, and scientific reasoningânamely, the absence of systematic evaluation of AI systems' capacity for scientific self-evaluation.

The proposed methodology combines synthetic error injection with multi-domain evaluation across physics, biomedicine, and social science, testing four error types (statistical, causal, methodological, interpretive) at three severity levels (subtle, moderate, gross) using six uncertainty estimation methods (BNN variance, deep ensemble disagreement, verbalized confidence, logit-based probability, multiple sampling, and self-reflection confidence). The factorial design enables stratified analysis to identify not only whether uncertainty-based error detection works overall, but also where it succeeds and where it fails.

The framework is presented as a research protocol rather than a completed study. The deep literature review, experimental execution, and verified citation stages remain pending, and all hypotheses are provisional. Nevertheless, the framework is designed to be directly executable, with clearly specified success criteria, statistical analysis plans, and figure generation protocols. It is intended to serve as a reference architecture for subsequent research stages and as a contribution to the emerging field of AI scientific self-evaluation.

The question posed in the titleâcan AI reliably identify when its own scientific conclusions are wrong?âremains open. The framework presented here provides a rigorous, multi-domain methodology for beginning to answer it. Whether the answer is affirmative, negative, or conditional on domain and error type, the results will advance our understanding of AI capabilities and limitations in scientific reasoning, and will inform the design of more reliable, self-aware AI systems for scientific research.
